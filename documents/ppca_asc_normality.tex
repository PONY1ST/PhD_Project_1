\chapter{Low Dimensional Asymptotic Normality}

\section{Basic Setup}\label{sec:basic_setup}

Given a "high dimensional" data set $\{\bm{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n$
where $\bm{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ for all $i = 1, \ldots, n$,
the statistician fixes a positive integer $q < p$ 
and wishes to find the $q$ principal components.
Assuming that the data is centred (that is, with mean $\bm{0}$),
the probabilistic principal component analysis model
is a statistical model that
treats $\bm{x}_i$'s as realizations 
of independent random variables $\bm{X}_i = \bm{W}\bm{Z}_i + \bm{\epsilon_i}$,
where $\bm{W}$ is an unknown $p \times q$ loading matrix of full rank, 
$\bm{Z}_i \sim N_q\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{I}_q}$ is the latent normal variable,
and $\bm{\epsilon_i} \sim N_p\bracket{\bm{0}, \sigma^2\bm{I}_p}$
is the Gaussian noise with unknown standard deviation $\sigma > 0$,
and $(\bm{Z}_1, \ldots, \bm{Z}_n, \bm{\epsilon}_1, \ldots, \bm{\epsilon}_n)$
are all independent (for now). 
Put $\theta = (\bm{W}_{\theta}, \sigma_{\theta}^2)$ for the unknown 
parameter to estimate
and $\Theta$ for all possible choices of $\theta$.
$\Theta$ is thus the collection of all tuples $(\bm{W}, \sigma^2)$
such that $\bm{W}$ is a real $p \times q$ matrix with $\text{rank}(\bm{W}) = q$
and $\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)$.
Given that the collection of all real $p \times q$ matrix with rank $q$
is an open subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{pq}$,
we consider $\Theta$ as an open subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{pq+1}$
and our statistical model is 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:original_model}
  \mathcal{P} = \curly{N_p\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{W}_{\theta}\bm{W}_{\theta}^T + \sigma_{\theta}^2\bm{I}_p}}_{\theta \in \Theta}.
\end{equation}

To resolve the non-identifiability of $\mathcal{P}$,
we introduce the quotient manifold structure.
For $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ and $(\bm{B}, t) \in \Theta$,
we define $(\bm{A},s) \sim (\bm{B}, t)$
to mean that they give rise to the same covariance matrix,namely when
$\bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p = \bm{B}\bm{B}^T + t\bm{I}_p$.
The equivalence classes are denoted by $[(\bm{A},s)]$
or more briefly $[\bm{A},s]$. 
Unless otherwise mentioned, $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ is the default representative 
we pick when we write an equivalence class as $[\bm{A},s]$.
Writing $O(q)$ for the collection of all $q \times q$ real orthogonal matrices,
it was shown in Datta's thesis that 
$[\bm{A},s] = \{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s) \mid \bm{Q} \in O(q)\}$.
Therefore, $M := \Theta / \sim$ can be identified as the orbits 
of the right action of $O(q)$ on $\Theta$
and, given that this Lie group action is smooth, proper, and free,
the quotient manifold theorem (see \cite{Lee_2013}, Theorem 21.10) shows that there is a unique smooth 
structure making $M$ a manifold of dimension $pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$ and 
the map $\pi: \Theta \to M$ 
defined by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pi_def}
  \pi((\bm{A},s)) = [\bm{A},s]
\end{equation}
is a smooth submersion.
Moreover, this action is verticle, transitive on fibers 
and is isomertic with respect to the Euclidean metric.
Thus, there exists a unique metric $g$ on $M$ making
$(M,g)$ a Riemannian manifold and $\pi$ a Riemannian submersion
(see \cite{Lee_2018}, Theorem 2.28), meaning that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:g_def}
  g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{V},c), (\bm{U},b)}
  = g_{[\bm{A},s]} \bracket{d\pi_{(A,s)}((\bm{V},c)), d\pi_{(A,s)}((\bm{U},b))},
\end{equation}
where $g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}$ is the standard Euclidean metric given by 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euclidean_metric}
  g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{V},c), (\bm{U},b)} = \tr(\bm{V}\bm{U}^T) + cb,
\end{equation}
whenever $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$, $(\bm{V},c)$, 
$(\bm{U},b) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta \cong \mathbb{R}^{p \times q} \times \mathbb{R}$.
For later convenience,
let us define two functions $\Phi: \Theta \to \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$
and $\bm{\Sigma}: M \to \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$
by setting, for every $[\bm{A},s] \in M$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_Phi_def}
  \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s]) := \Phi((\bm{A},s)) := \bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p.
\end{equation}
In other words, $\bm{\Sigma} \circ \pi = \Phi$
gives the covariance matrix.
\eqref{eq:original_model} is then reparametrized as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:quotient_model}
  \mathcal{P} = \curly{N_p\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}}_{[\bm{A},s]\in M}
\end{equation}
and this is an identifiable model we shall work with.

\section{Basic Facts about $(M,g)$}

Working with Riemannian submersion, 
we shall as usual start with computations regarding the horizontal and verticle 
tangent spaces.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:horizontal_verticle}
\,
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The map $\bm{\Sigma}$ defined in \eqref{eq:Sigma_Phi_def}
is a smooth injective immersion.
  \item At every $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$,
  the verticle tangent space 

  \begin{equation}\label{eq:verticle_space}
  V_{(\bm{A},s)} :=\ker(d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}) = \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})
  \end{equation}
  equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$,
with $\bm{\Omega}$ ranged over all $q \times q$ skew symmetric matrices.
  \item At every $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$,
  the horizontal tangent space $H_{(\bm{A},s)} := V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp}$
equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{V}, c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$
ranged over all $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and all $q \times q$ matrices $\bm{V}$
satisfying $\bm{V}^T \bm{A} = \bm{A}^T \bm{V}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix a point $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$
throughout this proof.
Before we start the proof,
let us first study the set $\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$,
where $\Phi$ is given in \eqref{eq:Sigma_Phi_def}.
For $(\bm{V}, c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$, 
define a curve $\gamma : (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to \Theta$
by setting $\gamma(t) = (\bm{A}+t\bm{V}, s + tc)$.
Then:

\begin{equation*}
  d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)} ((\bm{V}, c)) = \frac{d}{dt} \bigg|_{t = 0} \Phi(\gamma(t)) = \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p.
\end{equation*}
If $(\bm{V}, c) \in \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$,
then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ker_Phi_equation}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p = \bm{0}.
\end{equation}
If $\bm{u} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is any nonzero vector orthogonal to
all column vectors of $\bm{A}$, then $\bm{A}^T \bm{u} = \bm{0}$ and

\begin{equation*}
  0 = \bm{u}^T (\bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p) \bm{u}
  = c \, \bm{u}^T \bm{u}.
\end{equation*}
Given that $\bm{u} \neq \bm{0}$, we must have $c = 0$
and \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation} reduces to

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T = \bm{0}.
\end{equation}
Apply $\bm{u}$ to the right to get:

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T\bm{u} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Given that $\bm{A}$ has full rank, 
rank nullity theorem forces 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{V}^T\bm{u} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, we have proven that every vector $\bm{u}$
orthgonal to columns vectors of $\bm{A}$
will also be orthgonal to column vectors of $\bm{V}$.
This means $\bm{V}=\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$ for some 
$q \times q$ matrix $\bm{\Omega}$.
Substitute this back to \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced}
gives 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}(\bm{\Omega}^T + \bm{\Omega})\bm{A}^T = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Applying $(\bm{A}^T \bm{A})^{-1} \bm{A}^T$ 
to the left and $\bm{A}(\bm{A}^T \bm{A})^{-1}$ to the right 
yields $\bm{\Omega}^T + \bm{\Omega} = \bm{0}$.
Therefore, $\bm{\Omega}$ is skew-symmetric.
Conversely, \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced} 
is satisfied by $\bm{V}=\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$
with any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{\Omega}$.
We have proven that $\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$
equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$,
with $\bm{\Omega}$ ranged over all $q \times q$ skew symmetric matrices.

We now move back to prove 1 and 2 at the same time
by showing \eqref{eq:verticle_space}.
Clearly, $\bm{\Sigma}$ is well-defined 
and is injective.
The smoothness of $\bm{\Sigma}$
is guaranteed by relation $\bm{\Sigma} \circ \pi = \Phi$
(see for example \cite{Lee_2013} Theorem 4.29).
By the chain rule, we have 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:differential_Phi}
  d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)} = d \bm{\Sigma}_{[\bm{A},s]} \circ d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}.
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that 
$\ker(d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}) \subseteq \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$.
The other direction will follow if we can prove that 
$d \bm{\Sigma}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective.

Suppose that $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ is 
such that $d \bm{\Sigma}_{[\bm{A},s]} (\bm{v}) = 0$.
We prove that $\bm{v} = \bm{0}$.
Let $(\bm{V},c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$ 
be the horizontal lift of $\bm{v}$ at $(\bm{A},s)$,
meaning that $(\bm{V},c)$ is the 
unique horizontal tangent vector at $(\bm{A},s)$
such that
 $\bm{v} = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{V},c))$.
\eqref{eq:differential_Phi} then implies that 
$(\bm{V},c) \in \ker(d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$
and the proceeding discussion shows that 
$c = 0$ and $\bm{V} = \bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$ 
for some skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$.
To proceed, 
we look at the 
curve $\gamma: (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to \Theta$
defined by $\gamma(t) = (\bm{A}e^{t \bm{\Omega}}, s)$.
Clearly $\gamma(0)=(\bm{A}, s)$ and $\gamma'(0) = (\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)$.
Moreover, the matrix $e^{t \bm{\Omega}}$ is always orthogonal 
for any $t$ and any skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$ 
so that $\pi(\gamma(t)) = [\bm{A},s]$ for all $t$.
We have 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{v} =
  d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)) = 
  d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)} (\gamma'(0)) = \frac{d}{dt} \bigg|_{t=0} \pi(\gamma(t)) = \bm{0} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $d \bm{\Sigma}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective and
the verticle tangent space is the same as $\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$.
Since $[\bm{A},s]$ is arbitrary, $\bm{\Sigma}$ is an immersion.

To compute the horizontal tangent space, any $(\bm{V}, c) \in V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp}$ 
will satisfy 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:orthogonal_horizontal}
  0 = g_{(\bm{A},s)} \bracket{(\bm{V}, c), (\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)} = \tr(\bm{A}^T \bm{V} \bm{\Omega})
\end{equation}
for all skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$.
Decomposing $\bm{A}^T \bm{V} = \bm{S} + \bm{K}$, 
where $\bm{S}$ is the symmetric part and $\bm{K}$ is the skew-symmetric part,
we find out that 

\begin{equation*}
  0 = \tr(\bm{S} \bm{\Omega}) + \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}) = 0 + \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}) = \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}).
\end{equation*}
Setting $\bm{\Omega} = \bm{K}^T$ will yield that $\bm{K} = \bm{0}$.
Therefore, $\bm{A}^T \bm{V}$ is a symmetric matrix.
Conversely, any $\bm{V}$ such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{V}$ is symmetric will satisfy 
\eqref{eq:orthogonal_horizontal}. 
The proof is complete.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:exponential_map}
Given $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ and $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$, 
geodesics in $M$ starting at $[\bm{A},s]$ with initial velocity 
$\bm{v}$ takes the format of curves 
$\gamma: I \to M$, where $I$ is an open interval containing $0$,
defined by $\gamma(t) = [\bm{A} + t\bm{V}, s + tc]$,
where $(\bm{V},c) \in H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ is the horizontal lift 
of $\bm{v}$ at $ (\bm{A},s)$.
Consequently, the exponential map 
is computed as $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}) = [\bm{A} + \bm{V}, s + c]$,
at least well-defined over an open ball $\mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
of $\bm{0} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M \cong \mathbb{R}^{pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}}$ 
of radius $\min(\sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A}),s)$,
where $\sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A})$ denotes the smallest singular value of $\bm{A}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First of all, let us quickly check that the curve $\gamma$ is well defined.
Indeed, if $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$, where $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$, is another representative of $[\bm{A},s]$,
let $(\bm{V}',c')$ be the horizontal lift of $\bm{v}$ to $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$.
With the function $R_{\bm{Q}}: \Theta \to \Theta$ defined by $R_{\bm{Q}}((\bm{A},s)) = (\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$,
we have 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{v} = d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)} (\bm{V},c) = d(\pi \circ R_{\bm{Q}})_{(\bm{A},s)} (\bm{V},c)
  = d \pi_{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)} (dR_{\bm{Q}})_{(\bm{A},s)}(\bm{V},c)
  = d \pi_{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)} (\bm{V}\bm{Q},c).
\end{equation*}
Thus, $\bm{V}' = \bm{V}\bm{Q}$ and $c'=c$.
Substitute this into the definition of $\gamma$ 
will yield the exact same curve.

A well-known property of 
geodesics in case of Riemannian submersion (see for example 
\cite{O_neill_1967} Section 2 Corollary 2 or \cite{Valencia_2021} Theorem 3.6)
is that any geodesic $c$ in $\Theta$ with initial velocity being horizontal 
will always have horizontal velocities and 
$\gamma = \pi \circ c$
will remain a geodesic in $M$.
Therefore, $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}) = [\bm{A} + \bm{V}, s + c]$
is well defined for all vectors $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
of small enough norm
making sure that $(\bm{A} + \bm{V}, s + c) \in \Theta$.
To have a quantified upper bound of the norm,
we cite
Weyl's perturbation inequality (Chapter 7.3 Problem 16 equation (7.3.15) of \cite{Horn_Johnson_2013})
to get

\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A} + \bm{V}) \geqslant \sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A}) - \sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{V})
  \geqslant \sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A}) - \abs{\bm{v}} > 0,
\end{equation*}
whenever $\abs{\bm{v}} < \min(\sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A}),s)$.
Here $\sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{V})$ denotes the largest singular value of $\bm{V}$,
who is smaller than the Euclidean norm of $\bm{V}$ and hence norm of $\bm{v}$.
Thus, for all $\bm{v} \in \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$, $\rank(\bm{A} + \bm{V}) = q$
and the exponential map is well-defined.
\end{proof}

To compute the logarithmic map, we need first preparatory lemmas that 
work out an orthogonal matrix based on given points in $M$.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:q_classification}
If $\bm{L}$ is an invertible $q \times q$ matrix
and $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$ are such that $\bm{L}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric,
then there exists matrices $\bm{R}\in O(q)$, $\bm{P} \in O(q)$, 
a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ whose diagonal entries are $1$ or $-1$,
and a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{S}$ containing the singular values 
of $\bm{L}$ in increasing order 
such that $\bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$
and $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let us start by applying the singular value decomposition 
of $\bm{L}$ to get matrices $\bm{R}'\in O(q)$, $\bm{P}' \in O(q)$
and $\bm{S}$ such that $\bm{L} = \bm{R}'\bm{S}(\bm{P}')^T$.
Substituting the given condition that $\bm{L}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric will yield that
$\bm{S}\bm{J} = \bm{J}^T\bm{S}$, where $\bm{J} := (\bm{P}')^T \bm{Q} \bm{R}'$.

Clearly $\bm{J} \in O(q)$.
The equation 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{S}^2 = \bm{S}\bm{J}\bm{J}^T\bm{S} = (\bm{S}\bm{J})(\bm{S}\bm{J})^T 
  = (\bm{J}^T\bm{S})(\bm{J}^T\bm{S})^T = \bm{J}^T\bm{S}^2\bm{J}
\end{equation*}
shows that 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{J}\bm{S}^2=\bm{S}^2\bm{J}.
\end{equation*}
Looking at each entry of both size,
we end up with 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{J}_{ij}(\bm{S}_{jj}^2-\bm{S}_{ii}^2) = 0
\end{equation*}
for $i,j = 1, \ldots q$.
Given that $\bm{L}$ is invertible, its singular values are all strictly positive.
Therefore, in fact we have 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:j_s_equation}
  \bm{J}_{ij}(\bm{S}_{jj}-\bm{S}_{ii}) = 0
\end{equation}
for all $i$ and all $j$ so that $\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{S}\bm{J}=\bm{J}^T\bm{S}$.
Invert $\bm{S}$ yields that $\bm{J}$ is actually symmetric.
Being a symmetric orthogonal matrix, $\bm{J}^2 = \bm{I}_q$ and the eigenvalues of $\bm{J}$ 
must be $1$ or $-1$.
Another look at \eqref{eq:j_s_equation} shows that $\bm{J}$ is 
a block-diagonal matrix, diagonal if and only if
all diagonal entries of $\bm{S}$ are distinct.
Furthermore, each block of $\bm{J}$ must still be orthogonal and symmetric.

We now diagonalize $\bm{J}$ based on the above information.
Suppose that $\bm{S} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{s}_i\bm{I}_{m_i}$,
where $\bm{s}_1 > \ldots > \bm{s}_k$ are the unique diagonal entries of 
$\bm{S}$ and each repeats for $m_i$ times.
Thus, $\bm{J}$ has $k$ blocks.
Write the $i$th block by $\bm{J}_i$ so that
$\bm{J} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{J}_i$.
The spectral theorem then allows us to orthogonally diagonalize 
$\bm{J}_i = \bm{K}_i\bm{D}_i\bm{K}_i^T$,
where 
$\bm{K}_i \in O(m_i)$ and
$\bm{D}_i$ is an $m_i \times m_i$ diagonal matrix with $1$ or $-1$ diagonal 
entry. Put $\bm{K} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{K}_i$
and $\bm{D} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{D}_i$.
Then $\bm{K} \in O(q)$, $\bm{D}$ is a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix 
with $1$ or $-1$ diagonal entry, and $\bm{J} = \bm{K}\bm{D}\bm{K}^T$.
This involved construction leads us to the outcome that 
$\bm{K}$ in fact commutes with $\bm{S}$:

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{K}\bm{S} = \bigoplus_{i=1}^k \bm{s}_i\bm{K}_i = \bm{S}\bm{K}.
\end{equation*}
Put $\bm{P} = \bm{P}'\bm{K}$,
$\bm{R} = \bm{R}'\bm{K}$.
We have 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{Q} = \bm{P}' \bm{J} (\bm{R}')^T = \bm{P}' \bm{K}\bm{D}\bm{K}^T (\bm{R}')^T
  = \bm{P} \bm{D} \bm{R}^T
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T = 
  \bm{R}'\bm{K} \bm{S}\bm{K}^T(\bm{P}')^T
  = \bm{R}'\bm{S} \bm{K}\bm{K}^T(\bm{P}')^T
  = \bm{R}'\bm{S} (\bm{P}')^T =\bm{L}.
\end{equation*}
This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:singular_PR}
If $\bm{L}$ is an invertible $q \times q$ matrix
with singular value decomposition $\bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$,
then 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{P}\bm{R}^T = (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{L}^T.
\end{equation*}
This means, although neither $\bm{P}$ nor $\bm{R}$ is uniquely determined,
$\bm{P}\bm{R}^T$ is uniquely determined.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Given that

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{L}^T\bm{L} = \bm{P}\bm{S}^2\bm{P}^T,
\end{equation*}
we have
\begin{equation*}
  (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}} = \bm{P}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T.
\end{equation*}
Therefore:
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{P}^T\bm{P}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T = \bm{R}\bm{P}^T (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
The result follows from inverting $(\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}}$ and take transpose.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:logarithmic_map}
For every $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ there exists an open
neighborhood $U \subset M$ containing $[\bm{A},s]$ such that

\begin{enumerate}
  \item For every $[\bm{B},t] \in U$, $\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.
  \item The logarithmic map $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}:U \to T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ 
  is well-defined and is smooth in $U$. In fact:

  \begin{equation*}
    \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})-\bm{A}, t-s)),
  \end{equation*}
  where 

  \begin{equation*}
    \bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B}) 
    = (\bm{B}^T \bm{A} \bm{A}^T \bm{B})^{- \frac{1}{2}} \bm{B}^T \bm{A} \in O(q).
  \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First of all, 
if $\bm{R} \in O(q)$ and $\bm{P} \in O(q)$,
then $\bm{Q}(\bm{A}\bm{P},\bm{B}\bm{R}) = \bm{R}^T\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})\bm{P}$
and the same computation as in the proof of 
Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map}
shows that the formula of logarithmic map is indeed well-defined.

Given that the determinant is a continuous function
and $\det(\bm{A}^T\bm{A}) > 0$,
there exists a neighborhood $U_{\Theta} \subset \Theta$ 
containing $(\bm{A},s)$ 
such that $\det(\bm{A}^T\bm{B}) > 0$ 
whenever $(\bm{B},t) \in U_{\Theta}$.
Thus,
for any $[\bm{B},t] \in U_1 := \pi(U_{\Theta}) \subset M$,
$\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.

Next, 
it is a well-known fact that $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ 
is a local diffeomorphism 
and thus there exists an open neighborhood $U_2 \subset M$ containing $[\bm{A},s]$
such that the logarithmic map $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
is well-defined and is smooth in $U_2$.

We now compute $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
for $[\bm{B},t] \in U := U_1 \cap U_2$.
Suppose that $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ 
satisfies $\bm{v} = \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t])$.
By Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map},
$[\bm{B},t] = [\bm{A} + \bm{V},s+c]$,
where $(\bm{V}, c)$ is the horizontal lift of $\bm{v}$ to $(\bm{A}, s)$.
It follows that $c = t-s$ and that
there exists a $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$
such that $\bm{V} = \bm{B}\bm{Q}-A$.
Or equivalently:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithmic_general_in_proof}
  \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A}, t-s)).
\end{equation}
Given that $(\bm{V}, c)$ is a horizontal tangent vector, 
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle} shows that 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}^T(\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A}) = (\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A})^T \bm{A}
\end{equation*}
and consequently $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric.
Lemma~\ref{thm:q_classification} with $\bm{L} = \bm{A}^T \bm{B}$
shows the existence of $\bm{R}$, $\bm{P} \in O(q)$,
a diagonal matrix $\bm{S}$ with the singular values of $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$
in increasing order, and a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$
with diagonal entries $1$ or $-1$
such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$
and $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$.
\eqref{eq:logarithmic_general_in_proof} now becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithmic_PR_in_proof}
  \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T-\bm{A}, t-s)).
\end{equation}
Now let $\bm{B} \to \bm{A}$ and $t \to s$.
The left hand size of \eqref{eq:logarithmic_PR_in_proof}
now becomes $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{A},s]) = \bm{0} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
The singular value decomposition converges to 
the orthogonal diagonalization because $\bm{A}^T \bm{A}$
is symmetric, meaning that $\bm{P} =  \bm{R}$. Thus

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^T-\bm{A} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^T = \bm{I}_q$
and $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$.
Lemma~\ref{thm:singular_PR} then shows 
that 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{R}^T = (\bm{B}^T \bm{A} \bm{A}^T \bm{B})^{- \frac{1}{2}} \bm{B}^T \bm{A}.
\end{equation*}
This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Unfortunately, the logarithmic map is not globally defined
in the standard way
even over all $[\bm{B},t]$ such that $\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.
For a counterexample, take $p = 2$, $q = 1$,
$\bm{A} = [1,0]^T$, $\bm{B} = [1,1]^T$ and $s = t = 1$.
In this case, $O(1) = \{1,-1\}$. Then, 
although $\bm{A}^T\bm{B} = 1$ is invertible,
we still have 

\begin{equation*}
  \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))}
  = [\bm{B},1] = [-\bm{B},1]
  = \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((-\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))}.
\end{equation*}
The reason is that,
even when we assume $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible, 
deducing $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ 
relies on the local smoothness of the logarithmic map and the 
procedure of letting $[\bm{B},t] \to [\bm{A},s]$.
If $[\bm{B},t]$ is far away from $[\bm{A},s]$,
then $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$ with some $\bm{D} \neq \bm{I}_q$
can still produce a valid horizontal vector whose image under
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is $[\bm{B},t]$,
so that $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ fails to be injective
and admits no globally defined inverse.

Nevertheless, the choice $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ has an interesting 
property of selecting the geodesic of minimal speed
and length when $[\bm{B},t]$ is any point 
such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible.
To see this, 
consider a general candidate $\bm{Q}' = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$ and
write $\bm{v'} = d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s))$,
so that $(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s)$ is the horizontal lift of $\bm{v'}$
at $(\bm{A},s)$ and $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v'}) = [\bm{B},t]$.
Let us compute the squared norm of $\bm{v'}$:

\begin{align*}
  & \tab[0.4cm] g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v'}, \bm{v'})
  = g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s), (\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s)} \\
  &= \tr\bracket{(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A})(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A})^T} + (t-s)^2 
  = \tr(\bm{B}\bm{B}^T) + \tr(\bm{A}\bm{A}^T) - 2\tr(\bm{S}\bm{D}) + (t-s)^2,
\end{align*}
where the last equality substituted
$\bm{A}^T\bm{B}\bm{Q}' = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$
and used the cyclic property of the trace.
The fact that
the diagonal entries of $\bm{S}$ are strictly positive
show that
$g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v'}, \bm{v'})$ is minimized precisely when $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$.
Given that the geodesic starting at $[\bm{A},s]$
with initial velocity $\bm{v'}$ reaches $[\bm{B},t]$ at time $1$ with constant speed
$\sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v'}, \bm{v'})}$, its length is $\sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v'}, \bm{v'})}$.
The choice $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ gives the slowest and hence shortest geodesic.
To illustrate,
let us go back to the counterexample in the previous paragraph.
Put $\bm{v}_{\pm} := d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\pm\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))$.
Direct computation shows $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}_+,\bm{v}_+) = 1$ and $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}_-,\bm{v}_-) = 5$
so that the option corresponding to $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_1=1$
gives the slowest speed.
Therefore, it is actually possible to define 
$\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
over all $[\bm{B},t]$ such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible
by manually asking it to output the minimal speed candidate 
and this definition will yield the same $\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})$
formula. 

However, the situation totally breaks down 
in case $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is not invertible.
Consider again $p =2$, $q=1$, $\bm{A} = [1,0]^T$, $s = t = 1$,
but this time $\bm{B} = [0,1]^T$.
Then $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}_+,\bm{v}_+) = g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}_-,\bm{v}_-) = 2$
and there are two distinct geodesics of the same speed 
so $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t])$
cannot be well-defined.

(Picture: there is a hole at $[0,0]^T$. 
Starting $\bm{A}= [1,0]^T$, as $\bm{B}= [0,1]^T$ is glued to $[0,-1]^2$,
$\bm{v}_+$ points from $[1,0]^T$ to $[0,1]^T$,
$\bm{v}_-$ points from $[1,0]^T$ to $[0,-1]^T$)

Given the above situation and (the expectation that) 
our work should not require a global definition,
we stick to the standard definition of logarithmic map
only defined locally around $[\bm{A},s]$.
\end{remark}

\section{Properties of the Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis Model}
We now show that the statistical model $\mathcal{P}$ 
given by \eqref{eq:quotient_model}
is \textit{differentiable in quadratic mean}
and has \textit{positive definite Fisher information operator} 
in the sense developed in \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, definition 
III.1 and III.2.

Let us introduce a few notations.
For each $[\bm{A},s] \in M$,
let

\begin{equation}\label{eq:p_def}
  p(\bm{x}; [\bm{A},s])
  = (2\pi)^{-\frac{p}{2}} \text{det}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])) \exp \bracket{- 
  \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T(\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s]))^{-1}\bm{x}}
\end{equation}
be the density of $N_p(\bm{0}, \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s]))$.
Define two maps $l_{[\bm{A},s]}$ and $r_{[\bm{A},s]}$
with domain $\mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]} \subset T_{[\bm{A},s]}M \cong \mathbb{R}^{pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}}$
and codomain $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)$ by setting,
for all $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_def}
  l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) = \log \bracket{p(\bm{x}; \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r_def}
  r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) = \sqrt{p(\bm{x}; \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:DQM}
$r_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is Fréchet differentiable at any $\bm{v} \in \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$.
In particular, $\mathcal{P}$ is differentiable in quadratic mean with score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score}
  s(\bm{x}; [\bm{A},s])(\bm{h})
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr \curly{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}
  \squared{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1} \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_directional}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])
  := \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(t\bm{h}))
  = \bm{U}\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{U}^T + b  \bm{I}_p,
\end{equation}
if $\bm{h}\in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ lifts to $(\bm{U},b)$ at $(\bm{A},s)$.
Furthermore, $\mathcal{P}$ is a regular model in the sense that
the Fisher information operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G_def}
  G_{[\bm{A},s]} (\bm{h}_1,\bm{h}_2)
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr \curly{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}_1}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}_2}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}
\end{equation}
 is positive definite.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof for the Fréchet differentiability
is long but routine.
We compute the Gâteaux derivative
of $r_{[\bm{A},s]}$ at $\bm{v} \in \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
in a given direction $\bm{h} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
and show that the Gâteaux derivative is the Fréchet derivative 
by a direct analysis of the remainder term.
At the very end, we specialize our computations to 
$\bm{v} = \bm{0}$ to prove \eqref{eq:score}
and \eqref{eq:G_def}.

Given $\bm{v}$ and $\bm{h}$,
there exists an $\epsilon > 0$ such that
$\bm{v} + t\bm{h} \in \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
whenever $t \in (-\epsilon, \epsilon)$.
If they respectively lift to $(\bm{V},c)$
and $(\bm{U},b)$ at $(\bm{A},s)$,
then $\bm{v} + t\bm{h}$ lifts to $(\bm{V} + t\bm{U},c+tb)$
at $(\bm{A},s)$.
First of all:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_derivative}
  \begin{split}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})) & :=
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h}))
  \\ & = \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \curly{(\bm{A}+\bm{V} + t\bm{U})(\bm{A}+\bm{V} + t\bm{U})^T + (s+c+tb) \bm{I}_p}
  \\ & 
  = \bm{U}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V}\bm{U}^T + \bm{U}\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{U}^T + b  \bm{I}_p,
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
proving \eqref{eq:Sigma_directional} with special case $\bm{v}=\bm{0}$.
Secondly, for all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_derivative}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) :=
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h})(\bm{x})
  \\ & = \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \curly{-\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) 
  -\frac{1}{2}\log \det\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h}))}
  - \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h}))}^{-1}\bm{x} }
  \\ & =
  -\frac{1}{2}\tr 
  \squared{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h})) }}
  \\ & + \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v} + t\bm{h}))}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}\bm{x}
  \\ & =
  -\frac{1}{2} \tr 
  \squared{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})) }}
  \\ & + \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}\bm{x}
  \\ & = \frac{1}{2} \tr \curly{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \squared{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1} \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Finally:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r_derivative}
  \begin{split}
  D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})
  & = D_{\bm{h}} \exp \bracket{\frac{1}{2}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})}
  = \frac{1}{2} \exp \bracket{\frac{1}{2}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})} D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})
  \\ & = \frac{1}{2} r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
To prove that \eqref{eq:r_derivative}
indeed defines a valid Gâteaux derivative,
we must prove that $D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}) \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)$.
Luckily, the expectation and covariance of normal 
quadratic forms have already been very well studied in 
\cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000}.
For $\bm{X} \sim N_p(\bm{0}, \bm{\Sigma})$,
Theorem 7.7.1(i) 
states that 
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E} \squared{\bm{X}^T \bm{Z} \bm{X}}
  = \tr \bracket{\bm{Z}\bm{\Sigma}}
\end{equation*}
for any $p \times p$ matrix $\bm{Z}$
and 
Theorem 7.7.2 states that 
\begin{equation*}
  \text{Cov} \bracket{\bm{X}^T \bm{Z} \bm{X}, \bm{X}^T \bm{Y} \bm{X}}
  = \tr(\bm{Z}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Y}^T\bm{\Sigma}) + \tr(\bm{Z}^T\bm{\Sigma}\bm{Y}^T\bm{\Sigma})
\end{equation*} 
for any two $p \times p$ matrices $\bm{Y}$ and $\bm{Z}$.
Therefore, 
we can confirm without effort that the expectation of derivative of log density is zero:
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})
  p(\bm{x}; \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})))\mathrm{d} \bm{x}
   \\ & = -\frac{1}{2} \tr 
  \squared{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})) }}
  \\ & + \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^p}\squared{\bm{x}^T\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}\bm{x}} 
  p(\bm{x}; \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})))\mathrm{d} \bm{x} = 0
  \end{split}
\end{equation*} 
and, whenever $\bm{h}_1$ and $\bm{h}_2 \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher_computation}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] 	
   \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} D_{\bm{h}_1}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})
   D_{\bm{h}_2}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})
  p(\bm{x}; \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})))\mathrm{d} \bm{x}
  \\ & = \frac{1}{4}\text{Cov}\bracket{\bm{X}^T\bm{Z}_1\bm{X}, \, \bm{X}^T\bm{Z}_2\bm{X}}
  = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2 \tr \bracket{\bm{Z}_1 \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))
  \bm{Z}_2 \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}
  \\ & = \frac{1}{2}\tr \curly{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}_1}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}_2}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{X} \sim N_p(\bm{0}, \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})))$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Z_j_def}
  \bm{Z}_j := \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}_j}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
\end{equation}
is symmetric for $j=1,2$, so that the two terms in Theorem 7.7.2
of \cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000} coincide.
Thus, in the particular case $\bm{h} = \bm{h}_1 = \bm{h}_2$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r_L2norm}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] 	\|D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|^{2} =
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \squared{D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})}^2 \mathrm{d} \bm{x}
   \\ & = \frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})}^2
  p(\bm{x}; \bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})))\mathrm{d} \bm{x}
  = \frac{1}{8} \tr \curly{\squared{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))}^2}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation} 
Therefore, $D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}) \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)$
and \eqref{eq:r_derivative} is indeed a 
valid Gâteaux derivative at $\bm{v}$ in the direction $\bm{h}$.

To prove that this is indeed the Fréchet derivative,
let us look at the remainder term:

\begin{equation*}
  R(\bm{v}, \bm{h})(\bm{x}) := 
  r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+\bm{h})(\bm{x}) - 
  r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) - D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) 
\end{equation*}
and prove that 
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{\bm{h} \to \bm{0}} \frac{1}{\abs{\bm{h}}^2}
  \|R(\bm{v}, \bm{h});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|^2 = 0.
\end{equation*}
To start, the fundamental theorem of calculus shows that

\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+\bm{h})(\bm{x}) - 
  r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x}) 
  = \int_{0}^1 \frac{d}{dt} r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})(\bm{x}) \mathrm{d} t 
  \\ & = \int_{0}^1 \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+(t+s)\bm{h})(\bm{x})
  - r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})(\bm{x})}{s} \mathrm{d} t 
  = \int_{0}^1 D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})(\bm{x}) \mathrm{d} t.
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
Therefore,
by Minkowski's integral inequality and the linearity of $D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})$
in $\bm{h}$, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] 
  \|R(\bm{v}, \bm{h});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|
  = \|\int_{0}^1 \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})(\bm{x})- D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})} 
  \mathrm{d} t;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|
  \\ & \leqslant \int_{0}^1 
  \|D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| \mathrm{d} t
  = \abs{\bm{h}} \int_{0}^1 
  \|D_{\frac{\bm{h}}{\abs{\bm{h}}}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\frac{\bm{h}}{\abs{\bm{h}}}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| \mathrm{d} t
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
and 
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] \frac{1}{\abs{\bm{h}}^2}
  \|R(\bm{v}, \bm{h});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|^2 \leqslant
  \curly{\int_{0}^1 
  \|D_{\frac{\bm{h}}{\abs{\bm{h}}}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\frac{\bm{h}}{\abs{\bm{h}}}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| \mathrm{d} t}^2
  \\ & \leqslant 
  \curly{\int_{0}^1 \sup_{\abs{\bm{u}} = 1}
  \|D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| \mathrm{d} t}^2.
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
The proof will be complete once we can show that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:int_sup_u}
  \lim_{\bm{h} \to \bm{0}} 
  \int_{0}^1 \sup_{\abs{\bm{u}} = 1}
  \|D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| \mathrm{d} t = 0.
\end{equation}
We mus therefore examine the continuity of $D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})$,
which is easy to see if we revisit the formulas.
\eqref{eq:Sigma_derivative} shows that $ D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}(\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}))$
is smooth in both $\bm{h}$ and $\bm{v}$.
Thus,
\eqref{eq:r_derivative} shows that,
for each fixed $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$,
$D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})(\bm{x})$ 
is smooth in both $\bm{h}$ and $\bm{v}$
and \eqref{eq:r_L2norm}
shows that $\|D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|$
is smooth in both $\bm{h}$ and $\bm{v}$.
Combine the two facts, we see that 
$D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})$, as a map to $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)$,
is continuous in $\bm{h}$ and in $\bm{v}$.
The integrand inside \eqref{eq:int_sup_u}
is thus bounded uniformly 
over all $t \in [0,1]$ and all $\bm{h}$
in a compact neighborhood of $\bm{0}$
and it suffices to show 
that, for every $t \in [0,1]$, that 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lim_sup_u}
  \lim_{\bm{h} \to \bm{0}} 
  \sup_{\abs{\bm{u}} = 1}
  \|D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|  = 0,
\end{equation}
thanks to the bounded convergence theorem.
To prove \eqref{eq:lim_sup_u}, 
let $\bm{e}_1, \ldots, \bm{e}_d$ 
be a $g_{[\bm{A},s]}$ orthonormal basis
where $d = pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$.
Every unit vector $\bm{u}$ can be 
expressed as $\bm{u} = \sum_{i=1}^d u_i \bm{e}_i$
with $\sum_{i=1}^d u_i^2 = 1$.
By linearity of $D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})$ in $\bm{h}$, 
we have 
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] \|D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h}) - D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\| 
  \leqslant \sum_{i=1}^d \abs{u_i}\|D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h}) - 
  D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|
  \\ & \leqslant \sqrt{d} \max_{i=1, \ldots, d} \|D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h}) - 
  D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|.
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
Taking supremum yields
\begin{equation*}
  \sup_{\abs{\bm{u}} = 1}
  \|D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h})- D_{\bm{u}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})
  ;L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|  \leqslant 
  \sqrt{d} \max_{i=1, \ldots, d} \|D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h}) - 
  D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v});L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)	\|.
\end{equation*}
Given that $D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v}+t\bm{h}) 
\to D_{\bm{e}_i}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v})$
in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p)$
as $\bm{h} \to \bm{0}$
for every $i$, every $t$, and every $\bm{v}$,
the proof of Fréchet differentiability is complete.

Finally,
the formulas \eqref{eq:Sigma_derivative}, \eqref{eq:l_derivative},
and \eqref{eq:r_derivative}
simplifies greatly when $\bm{v} = \bm{0}$:
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
  & \tab[0.4cm] D_{\bm{h}}r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{0})(\bm{x})
  = \frac{1}{2} r_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{0})(\bm{x}) D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{0})(\bm{x})
  \\ & = \frac{1}{4} \sqrt{p(\bm{x}; [\bm{A},s])}
  \tr \curly{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}
  \squared{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}}
  \bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1} \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
The score $s(\bm{x}; [\bm{A},s])(\bm{h})$
therefore equals to $D_{\bm{h}}l_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{0})(\bm{x})$
and is indeed given by \eqref{eq:score}.
The Fisher information operator \eqref{eq:G_def} is obtained 
by taking $\bm{v} = \bm{0}$ in \eqref{eq:fisher_computation}.
To show that $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is positive definite,
suppose that $\bm{h} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
is such that $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{h},\bm{h})=0$.
\eqref{eq:G_def} then reads that 

\begin{equation*}
  \tr \curly{ \squared{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^2 } = 0.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s]) = \bm{0}$.
Now \eqref{eq:Sigma_directional}
reads the same as \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation}
in the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle}.
We therefore get $(\bm{U},b) \in \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}) = V_{(\bm{A},s)}$.
However, being lifted from $\bm{h}$,
$(\bm{U},b)$ must also be a horizontal vector.
Therefore, $\bm{h} = \bm{0}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:LAN}
Suppose that $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ is the true parameter
so that each $\bm{X}_i$ has density $p(\, \cdot \,; [\bm{A},s])$ as in \eqref{eq:p_def}.
We have the local asymptotic normality expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LAN_expansion}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \log \frac{p\bracket{\bm{X}_i; \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{\frac{\bm{h}}{\sqrt{n}}}}}{p(\bm{X}_i; [\bm{A},s])}
  = \sum_{i=1}^n s(\bm{X}_i;[\bm{A},s])\bracket{\frac{\bm{h}}{\sqrt{n}}}
  - \frac{1}{2} G_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{h},\bm{h}) + o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
\end{equation}
whenever $n$ large enough that
$\frac{\bm{h}}{\sqrt{n}} \in \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A},s]}$.
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LAN_weak_convergence}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \log \frac{p\bracket{\bm{X}_i; \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{\frac{\bm{h}}{\sqrt{n}}}}}{p(\bm{X}_i; [\bm{A},s])}
  \to N \bracket{-\frac{1}{2}G_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{h},\bm{h}), \, G_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{h},\bm{h})}
\end{equation}
in distribution as $n \to \infty$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Given that $\mathcal{P}$ is differentiable in quadratic mean
and has positive definite Fisher operator,
this follows from Proposition III.1 in \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}.
\end{proof}

\section{Properties of the Maximum Likelihood Estimator of $\mathcal{P}$}\label{sec:MLE}
We now move to the core of the subject.
Throughout this section we fix a \textit{true parameter} $[\bm{A}_0,s_0] \in M$,
we assume that $\bm{X}_1, \bm{X}_2, \ldots$ are independent and identically
distributed random vectors with common density $p(\, \cdot \,;[\bm{A}_0,s_0])$
as in \eqref{eq:p_def}, and we abbreviate

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_0_def}
  \bm{\Sigma}_0 := \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A}_0,s_0]) = \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T + s_0\bm{I}_p,
  \qquad
  \bm{S}_n := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \bm{X}_i \bm{X}_i^T,
  \qquad
  d := pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2} = \dim M.
\end{equation}
We write $\rightsquigarrow$ for convergence in distribution and,
following \eqref{eq:g_def}, we abbreviate
$\abs{\bm{v}} := \sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{v},\bm{v})}$ for $\bm{v} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.

The whole point of the construction of Section~\ref{sec:basic_setup}
is that the naive statement ``$\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{A}}_n - \bm{A}_0)$
is asymptotically normal'' is not merely false but meaningless:
by non-identifiability the estimator $\hat{\bm{A}}_n$ produced by
any algorithm is only determined up to a right multiplication by
an element of $O(q)$, so that $\hat{\bm{A}}_n$ need not converge to $\bm{A}_0$
at all, and only its orbit can.
The correct object to look at is the intrinsic estimation error
$\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}([\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]) \in T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$
of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, which by
Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map} is the ordinary error
\textit{after optimal rotational alignment}.
The goal of this section is to prove that this tangent vector
is asymptotically linear in the sense of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026},
Lemma III.3, with influence operator $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}s(\,\cdot\,;[\bm{A}_0,s_0])$,
and consequently asymptotically normal with covariance
operator $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$.

It will be convenient to name the horizontal lift.
By Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle} and the definition
\eqref{eq:g_def} of $g$, the restriction of $d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}$
to $H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ is a linear isometry onto $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$;
we denote its inverse by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lift_def}
  \iota_{(\bm{A},s)} := \bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}\big|_{H_{(\bm{A},s)}}}^{-1}
  : T_{[\bm{A},s]}M \longrightarrow H_{(\bm{A},s)},
  \qquad
  \iota_{(\bm{A},s)}(\bm{h}) =: (\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}, b_{\bm{h}}),
\end{equation}
so that $\iota_{(\bm{A},s)}$ is a \textit{linear} isometry and
$\abs{\bm{h}}^2 = \tr(\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}^T) + b_{\bm{h}}^2$.
Finally, since $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is a positive definite bilinear form
on $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:DQM}, the map
$\bm{h} \mapsto G_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{h}, \, \cdot \,)$ is a linear isomorphism
from $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ onto its dual $T^*_{[\bm{A},s]}M$;
we write $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ for its inverse, characterized by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:G_inverse_def}
  G_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{G^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\varphi), \bm{h}} = \varphi(\bm{h})
  \qquad \text{for all } \varphi \in T^*_{[\bm{A},s]}M
  \text{ and all } \bm{h} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M.
\end{equation}
Since the score $s(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])$ of \eqref{eq:score} is a linear
functional on $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$, the composition
$G^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}s(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])$ is a genuine tangent vector.

\subsection*{The maximum likelihood estimator}

The log-likelihood of the model \eqref{eq:quotient_model}
is the function on $M$ given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:loglik}
  \mathbb{L}_n([\bm{A},s]) := \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(\bm{X}_i;[\bm{A},s])
  = -\frac{np}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log \det \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])
  - \frac{n}{2}\tr \bracket{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}\bm{S}_n};
\end{equation}
it depends on the sample only through $\bm{S}_n$ and it is genuinely
a function on the quotient $M$, since the right hand side depends on
$(\bm{A},s)$ only through $\bm{\Sigma}$.
This is precisely the reason why the model had to be quotiented
in the first place: $\mathbb{L}_n$ is exactly constant along the
fibers of $\pi$, so no maximizer of $\mathbb{L}_n$ on $\Theta$ can ever be
unique, whereas on $M$ it can.
The following classical computation identifies the maximizer.
We write $\lambda_1(\bm{S}) \geqslant \ldots \geqslant \lambda_p(\bm{S})$
for the eigenvalues of $\bm{S} \in \text{Sym}(p)$, the space of real symmetric
$p \times p$ matrices, repeated according to multiplicity, and
we let

\begin{equation}\label{eq:O_def}
  \mathcal{O} := \curly{\bm{S} \in \text{Sym}(p) : \lambda_q(\bm{S}) > \lambda_{q+1}(\bm{S})},
\end{equation}
which is an open subset of $\text{Sym}(p)$ because the eigenvalues depend
continuously on $\bm{S}$.
For $\bm{S} \in \mathcal{O}$ we write $\bm{P}_q(\bm{S})$ for the orthogonal
projector of $\mathbb{R}^p$ onto the sum of the eigenspaces of
$\lambda_1(\bm{S}), \ldots, \lambda_q(\bm{S})$; this is well defined
precisely because of the spectral gap in \eqref{eq:O_def}.

\begin{proposition}[Tipping--Bishop]\label{thm:TB}
Let $\bm{S} \in \mathcal{O}$ be positive definite and put
$\lambda_j := \lambda_j(\bm{S})$ and $\bm{P}_q := \bm{P}_q(\bm{S})$.
Then the function
\begin{equation*}
  [\bm{A},s] \longmapsto
  -\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log \det \bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])
  - \frac{1}{2}\tr \bracket{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A},s])}^{-1}\bm{S}}
\end{equation*}
attains its maximum over $M$ at one and only one point
$[\hat{\bm{A}},\hat{s}] \in M$, determined by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:TB_formula}
  \hat{s} = \frac{1}{p-q}\sum_{j=q+1}^p \lambda_j,
  \qquad
  \hat{\bm{A}}\hat{\bm{A}}^T = \bm{P}_q \bm{S} \bm{P}_q - \hat{s}\, \bm{P}_q.
\end{equation}
Concretely, $\hat{\bm{A}} = \bm{W}_q(\bm{\Lambda}_q - \hat{s}\bm{I}_q)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}$
for an arbitrary $\bm{R} \in O(q)$, where the columns of
$\bm{W}_q \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of
$\bm{S}$ associated with $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_q$
and $\bm{\Lambda}_q = \text{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is Section 3.2 together with Appendix A of \cite{Tipping_Bishop_1999}:
equations (7) and (8) there give the stationary points and equations
(17)--(20) of their Appendix A.2 show that the global maximum is attained
exactly when the $q$ \textit{largest} eigenvalues of $\bm{S}$ are retained.
Since $\lambda_q > \lambda_{q+1}$ the set of retained eigenvalues,
and hence the projector $\bm{P}_q$, is unambiguous, so the maximizer
is a single point of $M$.
The two descriptions agree because
$\bm{P}_q\bm{S}\bm{P}_q = \bm{W}_q\bm{\Lambda}_q\bm{W}_q^T$
and $\bm{P}_q = \bm{W}_q\bm{W}_q^T$.
Finally $[\hat{\bm{A}},\hat{s}]$ is really a point of $M$:
$\hat{s} > 0$ because $\bm{S}$ is positive definite, and
$\rank(\hat{\bm{A}}) = q$ because
$\hat{s} \leqslant \lambda_{q+1} < \lambda_q$
forces every diagonal entry of $\bm{\Lambda}_q - \hat{s}\bm{I}_q$
to be strictly positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:MLE}
Whenever $\bm{S}_n$ is positive definite and lies in $\mathcal{O}$,
we let $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n, \hat{s}_n] \in M$ denote the maximum likelihood
estimator, that is the unique maximizer of \eqref{eq:loglik} over $M$
supplied by Proposition~\ref{thm:TB} applied to $\bm{S} = \bm{S}_n$.
On the complementary event we set $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n, \hat{s}_n] := [\bm{A}_0,s_0]$;
Lemma~\ref{thm:generic_S} below shows that this convention is invoked
only on a null set as soon as $n \geqslant p$.
\end{definition}

\subsection*{Genericity of the sample covariance matrix}

Both the well-posedness of the estimation error and the consistency
of the maximum likelihood estimator rest on the behaviour of the
spectral projector $\bm{P}_q$, which we now record.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:riesz}
\,
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mathcal{O}$ is open and connected in $\text{Sym}(p)$.
  \item $\bm{P}_q : \mathcal{O} \to \text{Sym}(p)$ is real analytic.
  \item Let $\bm{U}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}$ have orthonormal columns
  and define $\phi : \mathcal{O} \to \mathbb{R}$ by
  $\phi(\bm{S}) := \det \bracket{\bm{U}_0^T \bm{P}_q(\bm{S})\bm{U}_0}$.
  Then $\phi$ is real analytic and $\phi^{-1}(\{0\})$ is a Lebesgue null
  subset of $\text{Sym}(p) \cong \mathbb{R}^{\frac{p(p+1)}{2}}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
1. Openness was already observed.
For connectedness, fix $\bm{S} \in \mathcal{O}$ and diagonalize
$\bm{S} = \bm{K}\text{diag}(\bm{\lambda})\bm{K}^T$ with $\bm{K} \in O(p)$
and $\bm{\lambda} = (\lambda_1(\bm{S}),\ldots,\lambda_p(\bm{S}))$
non-increasing; replacing one column of $\bm{K}$ by its negative
if necessary, which does not change $\bm{S}$, we may assume
$\det(\bm{K}) = 1$.
Let $\bm{\mu} := (2,\ldots,2,1,\ldots,1)$ with the value $2$ repeated
$q$ times, and consider first the path
$t \mapsto \bm{K}\text{diag}((1-t)\bm{\lambda}+t\bm{\mu})\bm{K}^T$
for $t \in [0,1]$.
The vector $(1-t)\bm{\lambda}+t\bm{\mu}$ is non-increasing, being a convex
combination of non-increasing vectors, so it lists the ordered eigenvalues,
and the gap at position $q$ equals
$(1-t)(\lambda_q(\bm{S})-\lambda_{q+1}(\bm{S})) + t > 0$;
the path therefore stays inside $\mathcal{O}$ and joins $\bm{S}$
to $\bm{K}\text{diag}(\bm{\mu})\bm{K}^T$.
Next, $SO(p)$ is path connected, so there is a path $u \mapsto \bm{K}(u)$
in $SO(p)$ from $\bm{K}$ to $\bm{I}_p$; the induced path
$u \mapsto \bm{K}(u)\text{diag}(\bm{\mu})\bm{K}(u)^T$
has constant spectrum $\bm{\mu}$, hence constant gap $1 > 0$ at position $q$,
stays in $\mathcal{O}$, and ends at $\text{diag}(\bm{\mu})$.
Every point of $\mathcal{O}$ is thus joined to the fixed point
$\text{diag}(\bm{\mu})$ by a path inside $\mathcal{O}$.

2. Fix $\bm{S}_* \in \mathcal{O}$, choose $c \in \mathbb{R}$ with
$\lambda_{q+1}(\bm{S}_*) < c < \lambda_{q}(\bm{S}_*)$
and let $\Gamma \subset \mathbb{C}$ be the positively oriented circle
of centre $\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1(\bm{S}_*) + c)$
and radius $\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1(\bm{S}_*) - c) + 1$,
so that $\Gamma$ encircles $\lambda_1(\bm{S}_*), \ldots, \lambda_q(\bm{S}_*)$
and no other eigenvalue of $\bm{S}_*$.
Put $\delta := \min_{z \in \Gamma}\text{dist}(z, \text{spec}(\bm{S}_*)) > 0$.
If $\bm{S} \in \text{Sym}(p)$ satisfies $\|\bm{S}-\bm{S}_*\|_2 < \delta$
then $z\bm{I}_p - \bm{S}$ is invertible for every $z \in \Gamma$,
Weyl's inequality keeps exactly $q$ eigenvalues of $\bm{S}$ inside $\Gamma$,
and diagonalizing $\bm{S}$ shows, by the residue theorem applied to each
eigenvalue separately, that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:riesz_formula}
  \bm{P}_q(\bm{S}) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z\bm{I}_p - \bm{S})^{-1}\mathrm{d}z.
\end{equation}
Expanding the resolvent in a Neumann series,
\begin{equation*}
  (z\bm{I}_p - \bm{S})^{-1}
  = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\squared{(z\bm{I}_p-\bm{S}_*)^{-1}(\bm{S}-\bm{S}_*)}^k
  (z\bm{I}_p-\bm{S}_*)^{-1},
\end{equation*}
a series which converges absolutely and uniformly in $z \in \Gamma$
whenever $\|\bm{S}-\bm{S}_*\|_2 < \delta$, since
$\|(z\bm{I}_p-\bm{S}_*)^{-1}\|_2 \leqslant \delta^{-1}$ there.
Each summand is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in the entries
of $\bm{S}-\bm{S}_*$ whose coefficients are continuous in $z$,
so integrating term by term over the compact contour $\Gamma$
exhibits $\bm{P}_q$ as a convergent power series in the entries of
$\bm{S}-\bm{S}_*$ on that ball.

3. Real analyticity of $\phi$ is immediate from 2, the determinant being
a polynomial.
Moreover $\phi$ does not vanish identically: taking
$\bm{S}_\star := \bm{I}_p + \bm{U}_0\bm{U}_0^T$, whose eigenvalues are
$2$ with multiplicity $q$, with eigenspace the column space of $\bm{U}_0$,
and $1$ with multiplicity $p-q$, we get $\bm{S}_\star \in \mathcal{O}$,
$\bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_\star) = \bm{U}_0\bm{U}_0^T$ and
$\phi(\bm{S}_\star) = \det(\bm{I}_q) = 1$.
By 1 the domain $\mathcal{O}$ is open and connected, so the zero set of the
non-trivial real analytic function $\phi$ is Lebesgue null in
$\text{Sym}(p)$ by \cite{Mityagin_2020}.
Finally $\text{Sym}(p)\setminus \mathcal{O}$ is itself Lebesgue null:
it is contained in the zero set of the discriminant of the characteristic
polynomial of $\bm{S}$, which is a polynomial in the entries of $\bm{S}$
and does not vanish at $\text{diag}(1,2,\ldots,p)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:generic_S}
Suppose $n \geqslant p$.
Then, almost surely, $\bm{S}_n$ is positive definite,
$\bm{S}_n \in \mathcal{O}$, and the $q \times q$ matrix
$\bm{A}_0^T \hat{\bm{A}}_n$ is invertible.
In particular the orthogonal matrix

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q_n_def}
  \bm{Q}_n := \bm{Q}(\bm{A}_0, \hat{\bm{A}}_n)
  = \bracket{\hat{\bm{A}}_n^T\bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T\hat{\bm{A}}_n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
  \hat{\bm{A}}_n^T \bm{A}_0 \in O(q)
\end{equation}
of Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map} is almost surely well defined.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first check that the law of $\bm{S}_n$ is absolutely continuous with
respect to the Lebesgue measure of $\text{Sym}(p)$.
Let $\bm{Y}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ be the matrix whose rows are
$\bm{X}_1^T, \ldots, \bm{X}_p^T$ and let $\bm{Y}_2$ collect the remaining
$n-p$ observations, so that
$\bm{S}_n = \frac{1}{n}\bracket{\bm{Y}_1^T\bm{Y}_1 + \bm{Y}_2^T\bm{Y}_2}$
and $\bm{Y}_1$ is independent of $\bm{Y}_2$ with a strictly positive
Lebesgue density on $\mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$.
Condition on $\bm{Y}_2$ and consider
$T(\bm{Y}) := \frac{1}{n}(\bm{Y}^T\bm{Y} + \bm{Y}_2^T\bm{Y}_2)$
on the open set of invertible $\bm{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$,
whose complement is Lebesgue null.
Its differential at $\bm{Y}$ is
$\bm{H} \mapsto \frac{1}{n}(\bm{H}^T\bm{Y} + \bm{Y}^T\bm{H})$,
which is onto $\text{Sym}(p)$: given $\bm{B} \in \text{Sym}(p)$,
the choice $\bm{H} = \frac{n}{2}\bm{Y}^{-T}\bm{B}$ returns $\bm{B}$.
Thus $T$ is a smooth submersion, and by the rank theorem
(\cite{Lee_2013}, Theorem 4.12) every point has a neighbourhood on which
$T$ is, up to diffeomorphisms, a linear projection
$\mathbb{R}^{p^2} \to \mathbb{R}^{\frac{p(p+1)}{2}}$.
Since preimages of Lebesgue null sets under such projections are
Lebesgue null by Fubini's theorem, and diffeomorphisms preserve
Lebesgue null sets, $T^{-1}(N)$ is Lebesgue null for every
Lebesgue null $N \subseteq \text{Sym}(p)$, after covering the domain
by countably many such neighbourhoods.
Hence the conditional law of $\bm{S}_n$ given $\bm{Y}_2$ charges no
Lebesgue null set, and the same holds for its mixture over $\bm{Y}_2$.

By Lemma~\ref{thm:riesz}, the set of $\bm{S} \in \text{Sym}(p)$ that
either fail to be positive definite with $p$ distinct eigenvalues,
or lie outside $\mathcal{O}$, or satisfy $\phi(\bm{S}) = 0$,
is Lebesgue null; here we take $\bm{U}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}$ to be
any matrix with orthonormal columns spanning the column space of $\bm{A}_0$,
which has dimension $q$ because $\rank(\bm{A}_0) = q$.
It follows that almost surely $\bm{S}_n$ is positive definite,
$\bm{S}_n \in \mathcal{O}$ and $\phi(\bm{S}_n) \neq 0$.

It remains to see that $\phi(\bm{S}_n)\neq 0$ is exactly the invertibility
of $\bm{A}_0^T\hat{\bm{A}}_n$.
Write $\bm{A}_0 = \bm{U}_0\bm{T}$ with $\bm{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{q\times q}$
invertible, which is possible since the columns of $\bm{U}_0$
form a basis of the column space of $\bm{A}_0$, and use the
representative $\hat{\bm{A}}_n = \bm{W}_q(\bm{\Lambda}_q-\hat{s}_n\bm{I}_q)^{\frac{1}{2}}$
of Proposition~\ref{thm:TB}; note that invertibility of
$\bm{A}_0^T\hat{\bm{A}}_n$ is unchanged if $\hat{\bm{A}}_n$ is replaced by
$\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{R}$ with $\bm{R} \in O(q)$, so it is a property of the
class $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$.
Since $\bm{\Lambda}_q - \hat{s}_n\bm{I}_q$ is invertible, we get
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}_0^T\hat{\bm{A}}_n \text{ is invertible}
  \iff \bm{U}_0^T\bm{W}_q \text{ is invertible}
  \iff \det\bracket{\bm{U}_0^T\bm{W}_q\bm{W}_q^T\bm{U}_0} \neq 0
  \iff \phi(\bm{S}_n) \neq 0,
\end{equation*}
where we used $\det(\bm{U}_0^T\bm{W}_q)^2
= \det(\bm{U}_0^T\bm{W}_q\bm{W}_q^T\bm{U}_0)$
and $\bm{W}_q\bm{W}_q^T = \bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Consistency and the aligned estimation error}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:consistency}
Almost surely, $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n] \to [\bm{A}_0,s_0]$ in $M$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\bm{P}_0$ for the orthogonal projector onto the column space of
$\bm{A}_0$, so that $\bm{P}_0\bm{A}_0 = \bm{A}_0$.
The eigenvalues of $\bm{\Sigma}_0 = \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T + s_0\bm{I}_p$
are $\sigma_j(\bm{A}_0)^2 + s_0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, q$
and $s_0$ with multiplicity $p-q$, where $\sigma_j$ denotes the singular values;
since $\rank(\bm{A}_0)=q$ we have $\sigma_q(\bm{A}_0) > 0$ and hence
$\lambda_q(\bm{\Sigma}_0) > s_0 = \lambda_{q+1}(\bm{\Sigma}_0)$.
Thus $\bm{\Sigma}_0 \in \mathcal{O}$ and $\bm{P}_q(\bm{\Sigma}_0) = \bm{P}_0$.

By the strong law of large numbers applied to each of the finitely many
entries, $\bm{S}_n \to \bm{\Sigma}_0$ almost surely.
As $\mathcal{O}$ is open and $\bm{P}_q$ is continuous on $\mathcal{O}$
by Lemma~\ref{thm:riesz}, almost surely $\bm{S}_n \in \mathcal{O}$ for
all large $n$ and $\bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n) \to \bm{P}_0$.
Since
$\bm{S}_n$ is eventually positive definite as well,
Proposition~\ref{thm:TB} applies and \eqref{eq:TB_formula} gives, almost surely,
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{s}_n = \frac{1}{p-q}\tr\bracket{(\bm{I}_p - \bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n))\bm{S}_n}
  \longrightarrow \frac{1}{p-q}\tr\bracket{(\bm{I}_p-\bm{P}_0)\bm{\Sigma}_0}
  = \frac{s_0}{p-q}\tr(\bm{I}_p - \bm{P}_0) = s_0
\end{equation*}
because $(\bm{I}_p-\bm{P}_0)\bm{\Sigma}_0 = s_0(\bm{I}_p-\bm{P}_0)$, and
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{\bm{A}}_n\hat{\bm{A}}_n^T
  = \bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n)\bm{S}_n\bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n) - \hat{s}_n\bm{P}_q(\bm{S}_n)
  \longrightarrow \bm{P}_0\bm{\Sigma}_0\bm{P}_0 - s_0\bm{P}_0
  = \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T,
\end{equation*}
using $\bm{P}_0\bm{\Sigma}_0\bm{P}_0 = \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T + s_0\bm{P}_0$.

It remains to convert this into convergence in $M$.
We claim that if $\bm{B}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$ satisfy
$\bm{B}_n\bm{B}_n^T \to \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T$, then
$\min_{\bm{Q}\in O(q)}\|\bm{B}_n\bm{Q}-\bm{A}_0\|_F \to 0$.
Indeed $\|\bm{B}_n\|_F^2 = \tr(\bm{B}_n\bm{B}_n^T)$ is convergent, hence
bounded.
If the claim failed there would be $\epsilon > 0$ and a subsequence
along which $\min_{\bm{Q}}\|\bm{B}_n\bm{Q}-\bm{A}_0\|_F \geqslant \epsilon$;
by boundedness we may extract a further subsequence with
$\bm{B}_n \to \bm{B}$, and then $\bm{B}\bm{B}^T = \bm{A}_0\bm{A}_0^T$.
This matrix has rank $q$, so $\rank(\bm{B}) = q$ and
$(\bm{B},s_0) \sim (\bm{A}_0,s_0)$;
by the description of the equivalence classes recalled in
Section~\ref{sec:basic_setup} there is $\bm{Q}_0 \in O(q)$ with
$\bm{B} = \bm{A}_0\bm{Q}_0$.
Consequently
$\min_{\bm{Q}}\|\bm{B}_n\bm{Q}-\bm{A}_0\|_F
\leqslant \|\bm{B}_n\bm{Q}_0^T - \bm{B}\bm{Q}_0^T\|_F
= \|\bm{B}_n-\bm{B}\|_F \to 0$
along that subsequence, a contradiction.

Applying the claim to $\bm{B}_n = \hat{\bm{A}}_n$ we obtain, almost surely,
matrices $\bm{Q}_n' \in O(q)$ with
$\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n' \to \bm{A}_0$; combined with $\hat{s}_n \to s_0$
this says $(\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n', \hat{s}_n) \to (\bm{A}_0,s_0)$ in
$\Theta$, and the continuity of $\pi$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  [\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]
  = \pi\bracket{(\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n', \hat{s}_n)}
  \longrightarrow \pi\bracket{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)} = [\bm{A}_0,s_0]
\end{equation*}
in $M$, as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{thm:consistency} recovers, for the independent and
identically distributed Gaussian sampling scheme, the strong consistency
result of \cite{Datta_Chakrabarty_2023} and \cite{Datta_2026}, Corollary 1,
which is proved there by a Wald-type argument valid under considerably
weaker assumptions.
The proof above is included because it is short once
Lemma~\ref{thm:riesz} is available, and because it makes the section
self-contained.
\end{remark}

We can now fix once and for all the normal chart in which the whole
analysis will be carried out.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:chart}
There exists $\rho \in \bracket{0, \min(\sigma_{\text{min}}(\bm{A}_0), s_0)}$
such that, writing
$B_{\rho} := \{\bm{u} \in T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M : \abs{\bm{u}} < \rho\}
\subset \mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$,
the map $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ restricted to $B_{\rho}$
is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset
$\mathcal{U}_0 := \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(B_{\rho}) \subseteq M$,
and such that $\mathcal{U}_0 \subseteq U$, where $U$ is the neighbourhood
supplied by Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map} at $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} the exponential map is defined on
$\mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$, and it is a local diffeomorphism at
$\bm{0}$, so there is an open neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}$ of $\bm{0}$
inside $\mathcal{E}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ that is mapped diffeomorphically onto
an open subset of $M$.
Since $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ is continuous and
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{0}) = [\bm{A}_0,s_0] \in U$,
we may pick $\rho > 0$ so small that
$B_{\rho} \subseteq \mathcal{N}$ and
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(B_{\rho}) \subseteq U$.
A restriction of a diffeomorphism to an open subset is again a
diffeomorphism onto its (open) image.
\end{proof}

We fix such a $\rho$ for the rest of the section, and define the
\textit{aligned estimation error}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:e_n_def}
  \bm{e}_n := \bracket{\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n - \bm{A}_0,\ \hat{s}_n - s_0}
  \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q} \times \mathbb{R},
  \qquad
  \hat{\bm{u}}_n := \begin{cases}
    \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}\bracket{[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]}
    & \text{on } \mathcal{A}_n, \\
    \bm{0} & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{Q}_n$ is as in \eqref{eq:Q_n_def} and
$\mathcal{A}_n := \curly{[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n] \in \mathcal{U}_0}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:error_welldefined}
For $n \geqslant p$ we have $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_n) \to 1$;
on $\mathcal{A}_n$ the vector $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ belongs to $B_{\rho}$
and its horizontal lift at $(\bm{A}_0,s_0)$ is exactly $\bm{e}_n$,
that is $\iota_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}(\hat{\bm{u}}_n) = \bm{e}_n$;
and $\hat{\bm{u}}_n \to \bm{0}$ almost surely.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{thm:generic_S} the matrix $\bm{Q}_n$ is almost surely well
defined for $n \geqslant p$, so $\bm{e}_n$ is.
By Proposition~\ref{thm:consistency} and the fact that $\mathcal{U}_0$ is
an open neighbourhood of $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$, almost surely
$[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n] \in \mathcal{U}_0$ for all large $n$;
in particular $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_n) \to 1$.
On $\mathcal{A}_n$ we have $\hat{\bm{u}}_n \in B_{\rho}$ by
Lemma~\ref{thm:chart}, and since
$\mathcal{U}_0 \subseteq U$ the formula of
Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map} applies and reads
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{\bm{u}}_n
  = d\pi_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}\bracket{\bracket{\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n - \bm{A}_0,\ \hat{s}_n - s_0}}
  = d\pi_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}(\bm{e}_n).
\end{equation*}
As observed in the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map},
$\bm{e}_n$ is a horizontal vector at $(\bm{A}_0,s_0)$, so
$\iota_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}(\hat{\bm{u}}_n) = \bm{e}_n$ by \eqref{eq:lift_def}.
Finally let $\epsilon \in (0,\rho)$.
Then $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(B_{\epsilon})$ is an open neighbourhood
of $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$, so almost surely
$[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$ lies in it for large $n$, and injectivity of
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ on $B_{\rho}$ then forces
$\abs{\hat{\bm{u}}_n} < \epsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Assumption III.2 of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026} postulates that the estimator
almost surely avoids the cut locus of the true parameter, so that the
logarithmic map may be applied to it, and
Proposition~\ref{thm:error_welldefined} is what plays that role here.
We cannot verify that assumption literally, because $M$ is incomplete and
the discussion following Proposition~\ref{thm:logarithmic_map} shows that
$\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ genuinely fails to be globally defined.
What Proposition~\ref{thm:error_welldefined} provides instead is the only
thing the asymptotic theory ever uses: an event of probability tending to
one on which the intrinsic error is well defined, together with the
explicit Procrustes description \eqref{eq:e_n_def} of it.
Since modifying a sequence of random vectors on a sequence of events of
vanishing probability affects neither its limit in probability nor its
limit in distribution, all statements below are insensitive to the
convention adopted off $\mathcal{A}_n$.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Asymptotic linearity}

We can now state the definition, which is Definition 3.10 of the
companion draft and, in the language of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026},
the hypothesis of their Lemma III.3.

\begin{definition}\label{def:asymptotic_linearity}
Let $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$ be a sequence of estimators for which the
intrinsic estimation error $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ of \eqref{eq:e_n_def} is
defined on events of probability tending to one.
The sequence is said to be \textit{asymptotically linear at $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$
with influence operator} $\text{IF}$
if $\text{IF} : \mathbb{R}^p \to T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$ is measurable,
centred and square integrable, that is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:IF_moments}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{\text{IF}(\bm{X}_1)} = \bm{0}
  \qquad \text{and} \qquad
  \mathbb{E}\squared{\abs{\text{IF}(\bm{X}_1)}^2} < \infty,
\end{equation}
and if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymptotic_linearity}
  \sqrt{n}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_n
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n \text{IF}(\bm{X}_i) + o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),
\end{equation}
the remainder being $o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)$ with respect to the norm
$\abs{\, \cdot \,}$ of $T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Both sides of \eqref{eq:asymptotic_linearity} live in the single fixed
vector space $T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$, so the expression is meaningful even
though $M$ is curved; this is exactly the device by which
\cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026} transport the classical theory to manifolds.
Note also that $\text{IF}$ is required to take values in the
\textit{tangent} space, whereas the score \eqref{eq:score} is a linear
functional, that is an element of the \textit{cotangent} space;
the inverse Fisher operator \eqref{eq:G_inverse_def} is precisely
what converts one into the other.
\end{remark}

The following theorem is the main result of this section.
It is the analogue of Theorem 5.3 of the companion draft, and it
instantiates Corollary V.1 of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026} for the
probabilistic principal component analysis model.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:MLE_CLT}
Let $1 \leqslant q < p$ and let $[\bm{A}_0,s_0] \in M$.
Then the maximum likelihood estimator $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$ of
Definition~\ref{def:MLE} is asymptotically linear at $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$
in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:asymptotic_linearity},
with influence operator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:IF_def}
  \text{IF}(\bm{x}) := G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}\bracket{s(\bm{x};[\bm{A}_0,s_0])}
  \in T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M .
\end{equation}
That is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MLE_linear}
  \sqrt{n}\,\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}\bracket{[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]}
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n
  G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}\bracket{s(\bm{X}_i;[\bm{A}_0,s_0])} + o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MLE_CLT}
  \sqrt{n}\,\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}\bracket{[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]}
  \rightsquigarrow N\bracket{\bm{0}, G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}}
  \qquad \text{on } T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M,
\end{equation}
the Gaussian measure on $T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$ whose covariance operator is
the inverse Fisher information operator.
Equivalently, in terms of the Procrustes aligned error \eqref{eq:e_n_def},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MLE_CLT_procrustes}
  \sqrt{n}\bracket{\hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n - \bm{A}_0,\ \hat{s}_n - s_0}
  \rightsquigarrow
  \iota_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}\bracket{N\bracket{\bm{0}, G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}}}
\end{equation}
as random elements of $\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\times \mathbb{R}$;
the limit is a centred Gaussian law carried by the
$d$-dimensional subspace $H_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $G := G_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$, $\iota := \iota_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}$
and $s(\bm{x}) := s(\bm{x};[\bm{A}_0,s_0])$ throughout.
The strategy is the one of Step 2 of the companion draft:
in the normal chart supplied by Lemma~\ref{thm:chart} the family
$\mathcal{P}$ becomes an ordinary parametric family indexed by an open
subset of a Euclidean space, and $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ becomes an ordinary
$M$-estimator, so that Theorem 5.23 of \cite{van_der_Vaart_1998}
applies once its four hypotheses have been verified.
The essential structural point, which makes every verification a finite
computation, is that by Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} the
exponential map is \textit{linear} in the horizontal lift, so no
approximation of $\text{Exp}$ is ever needed.

\textbf{Step 1: the model in normal coordinates.}
Fix a $g_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$-orthonormal basis $\bm{e}_1,\ldots,\bm{e}_d$ of
$T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$ and identify $T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$ with
$\mathbb{R}^d$ through it; under this identification $B_{\rho}$ is the
open Euclidean ball of radius $\rho$ and $\abs{\, \cdot \,}$ is the
Euclidean norm.
For $\bm{u} \in B_{\rho}$ put $(\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}, b_{\bm{u}}) := \iota(\bm{u})$
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_u_def}
  \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}} := \bm{\Sigma}\bracket{\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})}
  = (\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})(\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})^T
  + (s_0+b_{\bm{u}})\bm{I}_p,
\end{equation}
using Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} and \eqref{eq:Sigma_Phi_def}.
Because $\iota$ is linear, expanding \eqref{eq:Sigma_u_def} gives the
\textit{exact} identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_u_quadratic}
  \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}} = \bm{\Sigma}_0
  + \bracket{\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}\bm{A}_0^T + \bm{A}_0\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}^T + b_{\bm{u}}\bm{I}_p}
  + \bm{U}_{\bm{u}}\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}^T
  = \bm{\Sigma}_0 + D_{\bm{u}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A}_0,s_0])
  + \bm{U}_{\bm{u}}\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}^T,
\end{equation}
by \eqref{eq:Sigma_directional}: as a function of $\bm{u}$, the covariance
matrix is a quadratic polynomial with no remainder whatsoever.
Set, for $\bm{u} \in B_{\rho}$ and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m_def}
  m(\bm{u};\bm{x}) := \log p\bracket{\bm{x}; \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})}
  = -\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log\det \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}
  - \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\bm{x},
\end{equation}
which in the notation \eqref{eq:l_def} is $l_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})(\bm{x})$,
and write $\mathbb{M}_n(\bm{u}) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n m(\bm{u};\bm{X}_i)$
and $M(\bm{u}) := \mathbb{E}\squared{m(\bm{u};\bm{X}_1)}$.
By \eqref{eq:loglik} and Lemma~\ref{thm:chart},
on the event $\mathcal{A}_n$ the vector $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ maximizes
$\mathbb{M}_n$ over $B_{\rho}$, since $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$ maximizes
$\mathbb{L}_n$ over all of $M$ and lies in the chart.

\textbf{Step 2: uniform spectral bounds.}
Let $\bm{u} \in B_{\rho}$.
Since $\iota$ is an isometry, $\|\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}\|_F \leqslant \rho$
and $\abs{b_{\bm{u}}} \leqslant \rho$; as $\rho < s_0$, formula
\eqref{eq:Sigma_u_def} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral_bounds}
  c_1 \bm{I}_p \preceq \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}} \preceq c_2 \bm{I}_p,
  \qquad
  c_1 := s_0 - \rho > 0,
  \qquad
  c_2 := \bracket{\sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{A}_0)+\rho}^2 + s_0 + \rho,
\end{equation}
the lower bound because $(\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})(\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})^T
\succeq \bm{0}$, and the upper bound because
$\sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}) \leqslant
\sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{A}_0) + \|\bm{U}_{\bm{u}}\|_F$.
In particular $\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\|_2 \leqslant c_1^{-1}$
and $\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}\|_F \leqslant \sqrt{p}\,c_2$
uniformly on $B_{\rho}$.

\textbf{Step 3: smoothness and the derivative of the criterion.}
On the open cone of positive definite matrices the maps
$\bm{\Sigma}\mapsto \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$ and $\bm{\Sigma}\mapsto\log\det\bm{\Sigma}$
are real analytic, and $\bm{u}\mapsto\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}$ is a polynomial by
\eqref{eq:Sigma_u_quadratic}; hence for every fixed $\bm{x}$ the function
$\bm{u} \mapsto m(\bm{u};\bm{x})$ is $C^{\infty}$ on $B_{\rho}$.
Its differential is computed exactly as in \eqref{eq:l_derivative}: for
$\bm{h} \in T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:m_derivative}
  \nabla m(\bm{u};\bm{x})[\bm{h}]
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\curly{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
  \squared{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
  \bracket{D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}\bracket{\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})}}},
\end{equation}
where, by \eqref{eq:Sigma_derivative},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sigma_derivative_u}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}\bracket{\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})}
  = \bm{U}_{\bm{h}}(\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})^T
  + (\bm{A}_0+\bm{U}_{\bm{u}})\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}^T + b_{\bm{h}}\bm{I}_p,
\end{equation}
which is linear in $\bm{h}$ and affine in $\bm{u}$.
Taking $\bm{u} = \bm{0}$ in \eqref{eq:m_derivative} and comparing with
\eqref{eq:score} shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score_is_gradient}
  \nabla m(\bm{0};\bm{x})[\bm{h}] = s(\bm{x})(\bm{h}),
\end{equation}
so that the gradient of the criterion at the truth is the score.

\textbf{Step 4: a square integrable Lipschitz envelope.}
Let $\bm{u} \in B_{\rho}$ and let $\bm{h}$ be a unit vector.
From \eqref{eq:Sigma_derivative_u} and
$\|\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}\bm{C}^T\|_F \leqslant \|\bm{U}_{\bm{h}}\|_F\|\bm{C}\|_2$,
\begin{equation*}
  \left\|D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}\bracket{\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u})}\right\|_F
  \leqslant 2\bracket{\sigma_{\text{max}}(\bm{A}_0)+\rho} + \sqrt{p}
  =: c_3 .
\end{equation*}
Combining this with $\abs{\tr(\bm{Z}\bm{Y})}\leqslant \|\bm{Z}\|_F\|\bm{Y}\|_F$
and the bounds of Step 2, \eqref{eq:m_derivative} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope}
  \sup_{\abs{\bm{h}}=1}\abs{\nabla m(\bm{u};\bm{x})[\bm{h}]}
  \leqslant \frac{c_3}{2c_1^{2}}\bracket{\|\bm{x}\|^2 + \sqrt{p}\,c_2}
  =: \dot{m}(\bm{x})
  \qquad \text{for all } \bm{u} \in B_{\rho}.
\end{equation}
Since $\bm{X}_1$ is Gaussian, $\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}_1\|^4] < \infty$
and therefore $\mathbb{E}[\dot{m}(\bm{X}_1)^2] < \infty$.
As $B_{\rho}$ is convex and $m(\, \cdot \,;\bm{x})$ is $C^1$ there,
the mean value theorem yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lipschitz}
  \abs{m(\bm{u}_1;\bm{x}) - m(\bm{u}_2;\bm{x})}
  \leqslant \dot{m}(\bm{x})\abs{\bm{u}_1-\bm{u}_2}
  \qquad \text{for all } \bm{u}_1, \bm{u}_2 \in B_{\rho}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5: the population criterion.}
Since $\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}_1\bm{X}_1^T] = \bm{\Sigma}_0$, \eqref{eq:m_def} gives
the closed form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M_def}
  M(\bm{u}) = -\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log \det \bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}
  - \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_0},
\end{equation}
which, by the argument of Step 3, is $C^{\infty}$ on $B_{\rho}$;
in particular no differentiation under the integral sign is needed
anywhere below.
Subtracting $M(\bm{0})$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:KL}
  M(\bm{0}) - M(\bm{u})
  = \frac{1}{2}\squared{\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_0} - p
  + \log\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_0}}
  = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^p \bracket{\mu_j - 1 - \log\mu_j},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_p > 0$ are the eigenvalues of the positive definite
matrix $\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_0\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}$,
which is similar to $\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_0$.
The elementary inequality $\mu - 1 - \log \mu \geqslant 0$, with equality
only at $\mu = 1$, shows that $M(\bm{0}) - M(\bm{u}) \geqslant 0$ with
equality if and only if all $\mu_j = 1$, that is if and only if
$\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}} = \bm{\Sigma}_0$.
By the injectivity of $\bm{\Sigma}$ established in
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle} this happens exactly when
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}(\bm{u}) = [\bm{A}_0,s_0]$, and by
Lemma~\ref{thm:chart} exactly when $\bm{u} = \bm{0}$.
Hence $\bm{0}$ is the unique maximizer of $M$ over $B_{\rho}$.

We now compute the second order Taylor expansion of $M$ at $\bm{0}$.
Fix $\bm{h} \in T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M$, write
$(\bm{U},b) := \iota(\bm{h})$, and let
$\bm{\Sigma}(t) := \bm{\Sigma}_{t\bm{h}}$.
By \eqref{eq:Sigma_u_quadratic},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ray}
  \bm{\Sigma}(t) = \bm{\Sigma}_0 + t\bm{\Delta} + t^2 \bm{U}\bm{U}^T,
  \qquad
  \bm{\Delta} := D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A}_0,s_0])
  = \bm{U}\bm{A}_0^T + \bm{A}_0\bm{U}^T + b\bm{I}_p,
\end{equation}
so $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}(0) = \bm{\Delta}$ and
$\ddot{\bm{\Sigma}}(0) = 2\bm{U}\bm{U}^T$ exactly.
Using $\frac{d}{dt}\log\det\bm{\Sigma} = \tr(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}})$
and $\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1} = -\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$,
the two terms of \eqref{eq:M_def} contribute
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\squared{-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\bm{\Sigma}(t)}
  = -\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\bm{\Delta}},
  \qquad
  \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\squared{-\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(t)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_0}}
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\bm{\Delta}},
\end{equation*}
whose sum vanishes, so $\nabla M(\bm{0}) = 0$, as it must since $\bm{0}$ is
an interior maximizer.
Differentiating once more,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\bigg|_{t=0}\squared{-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\bm{\Sigma}(t)}
  = -\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\ddot{\bm{\Sigma}}(0)}
  + \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\bm{\Delta}}^2}
\end{equation*}
and, since
$\frac{d}{dt}\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}}
= \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\ddot{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
- 2\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\bigg|_{t=0}\squared{-\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(t)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_0}}
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\ddot{\bm{\Sigma}}(0)}
  - \tr\bracket{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\bm{\Delta}}^2}.
\end{equation*}
The terms in $\ddot{\bm{\Sigma}}(0)$ cancel identically and we are left with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hessian}
  \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\bigg|_{t=0}M(t\bm{h})
  = -\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}
  D_{\bm{h}}\bm{\Sigma}([\bm{A}_0,s_0])}^2}
  = -G(\bm{h},\bm{h})
\end{equation}
by \eqref{eq:G_def}.
As $M$ is twice continuously differentiable on $B_{\rho}$, its Hessian at
$\bm{0}$ is the symmetric bilinear form determined by the quadratic form
\eqref{eq:hessian}, namely $-G$, and Taylor's theorem gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M_taylor}
  M(\bm{u}) = M(\bm{0}) - \frac{1}{2}G(\bm{u},\bm{u}) + o\bracket{\abs{\bm{u}}^2}
  \qquad \text{as } \bm{u} \to \bm{0}.
\end{equation}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:DQM} the form $G$ is positive definite, so the second
derivative matrix of $M$ at $\bm{0}$ is symmetric and nonsingular.
Incidentally, \eqref{eq:hessian} is the manifold version of the classical
identity between the Fisher information and the expected negative Hessian
of the log-likelihood, recorded as Remark III.4 of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026};
here it comes out of \eqref{eq:Sigma_u_quadratic} by a finite computation
precisely because the exponential map is linear in the horizontal lift.

\textbf{Step 6: application of the $M$-estimation theorem.}
We check the four hypotheses of Theorem 5.23 of
\cite{van_der_Vaart_1998} for the criterion functions
$\{m(\bm{u};\, \cdot \,)\}_{\bm{u} \in B_{\rho}}$ indexed by the open
subset $B_{\rho}$ of $\mathbb{R}^d$, at the point $\bm{u} = \bm{0}$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each $\bm{u}$ the map $\bm{x}\mapsto m(\bm{u};\bm{x})$ is
  measurable, being continuous, and for each $\bm{x}$ the map
  $\bm{u}\mapsto m(\bm{u};\bm{x})$ is differentiable at $\bm{0}$ with
  derivative $s(\bm{x}) \in L^2$, by Step 3 and \eqref{eq:score_is_gradient};
  square integrability of the score is \eqref{eq:r_L2norm}.
  \item The Lipschitz condition with square integrable envelope is
  \eqref{eq:lipschitz} and \eqref{eq:envelope}.
  \item The map $\bm{u}\mapsto M(\bm{u})$ admits the second order Taylor
  expansion \eqref{eq:M_taylor} at the maximizer $\bm{0}$, with
  nonsingular symmetric second derivative matrix $-G$, by Step 5.
  \item By Proposition~\ref{thm:error_welldefined},
  $\hat{\bm{u}}_n \to \bm{0}$ in probability, and
  $\mathbb{M}_n(\hat{\bm{u}}_n) \geqslant \mathbb{M}_n(\bm{0})$ holds exactly:
  on $\mathcal{A}_n$ because $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ maximizes $\mathbb{M}_n$ over
  $B_{\rho} \ni \bm{0}$ by Step 1, and off $\mathcal{A}_n$ because there
  $\hat{\bm{u}}_n = \bm{0}$.
  In particular
  $\mathbb{M}_n(\hat{\bm{u}}_n) \geqslant \mathbb{M}_n(\bm{0}) - o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1})$.
\end{enumerate}
The cited theorem therefore yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vdV_conclusion}
  \sqrt{n}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_n
  = \bm{G}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n \bm{s}(\bm{X}_i) + o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),
\end{equation}
where, in the orthonormal coordinates of Step 1, $\bm{G}$ is the
$d \times d$ matrix with entries $\bm{G}_{jk} = G(\bm{e}_j,\bm{e}_k)$ and
$\bm{s}(\bm{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the vector with entries
$\bm{s}(\bm{x})_j = s(\bm{x})(\bm{e}_j)$.
Now if $\text{IF}(\bm{x}) = \sum_{j}c_j(\bm{x})\bm{e}_j$ is defined by
\eqref{eq:IF_def}, then \eqref{eq:G_inverse_def} reads
$\sum_k \bm{G}_{jk}c_k(\bm{x}) = s(\bm{x})(\bm{e}_j)$ for every $j$,
that is $\bm{c}(\bm{x}) = \bm{G}^{-1}\bm{s}(\bm{x})$;
so \eqref{eq:vdV_conclusion} is precisely
\eqref{eq:MLE_linear} and \eqref{eq:asymptotic_linearity}.

The requirements \eqref{eq:IF_moments} hold: the score has mean zero
and covariance $G$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:DQM}, that is
$\mathbb{E}[\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)] = \bm{0}$ and
$\mathbb{E}[\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)^T] = \bm{G}$,
whence $\mathbb{E}[\bm{c}(\bm{X}_1)] = \bm{0}$ and
$\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{c}(\bm{X}_1)\|^2] = \tr(\bm{G}^{-1}) < \infty$.
The vectors $\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1), \bm{s}(\bm{X}_2), \ldots$ being independent,
identically distributed, centred and square integrable, the multivariate
central limit theorem gives
$n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^n\bm{s}(\bm{X}_i) \rightsquigarrow N(\bm{0},\bm{G})$,
so that by \eqref{eq:vdV_conclusion}, Slutsky's lemma and the continuous
mapping theorem,
\begin{equation*}
  \sqrt{n}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_n \rightsquigarrow
  N\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{G}^{-1}\bm{G}\bm{G}^{-1}} = N\bracket{\bm{0},\bm{G}^{-1}},
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:MLE_CLT}: in the $g_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$-orthonormal basis
$\bm{e}_1,\ldots,\bm{e}_d$ the covariance operator $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$
is represented by the matrix $\bm{G}^{-1}$.

\textbf{Step 7: the Procrustes form.}
By Proposition~\ref{thm:error_welldefined},
$\bm{e}_n = \iota(\hat{\bm{u}}_n)$ on $\mathcal{A}_n$, and
$\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_n)\to 1$.
If $\bm{\xi}_n$ and $\bm{\eta}_n$ agree on events of probability tending
to one and $\bm{\xi}_n \rightsquigarrow \bm{\xi}$, then
$\bm{\eta}_n\rightsquigarrow \bm{\xi}$ as well, since for every bounded
Lipschitz test function $\psi$ one has
$\abs{\mathbb{E}\psi(\bm{\xi}_n)-\mathbb{E}\psi(\bm{\eta}_n)}
\leqslant 2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_n^c)\to 0$
by the portmanteau theorem.
Applying this and the continuous mapping theorem to the linear isometry
$\iota$ turns \eqref{eq:MLE_CLT} into \eqref{eq:MLE_CLT_procrustes};
the limit is carried by $\iota(T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M) = H_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}$,
which by Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle} has dimension $d$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:influence_relation}
The influence operator \eqref{eq:IF_def} satisfies the influence relation
of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, Lemma III.3, namely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:influence_relation}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{\text{IF}(\bm{X}_1)\otimes s(\bm{X}_1;[\bm{A}_0,s_0])}
  = \text{id}_{T_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}M}.
\end{equation}
Moreover it is the only influence operator with values in the span of the
score for which \eqref{eq:asymptotic_linearity} can hold.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In the coordinates of Step 6 of the previous proof,
\eqref{eq:influence_relation} reads
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{c}(\bm{X}_1)\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)^T}
  = \bm{G}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)\bm{s}(\bm{X}_1)^T}
  = \bm{G}^{-1}\bm{G} = \bm{I}_d .
\end{equation*}
For the uniqueness, an influence operator of the stated form is
$\text{IF}'(\bm{x}) = \bm{C}\bm{s}(\bm{x})$ for some $d\times d$ matrix
$\bm{C}$, and \eqref{eq:influence_relation}, which
\cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026} show to be forced for regular asymptotically
linear sequences, gives $\bm{C}\bm{G} = \bm{I}_d$, that is
$\bm{C} = \bm{G}^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Three comments on the content of Theorem~\ref{thm:MLE_CLT}.

First, \eqref{eq:MLE_CLT_procrustes} is the well posed replacement of the
false naive statement discussed at the beginning of this section:
it is not the raw error $\hat{\bm{A}}_n - \bm{A}_0$ that is asymptotically
Gaussian, but the error measured after the optimal rotational alignment
$\hat{\bm{A}}_n \mapsto \hat{\bm{A}}_n\bm{Q}_n$, and the limit lives on the
horizontal space $H_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}$ only.
Viewed inside $\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\times\mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}^{pq+1}$
the limiting Gaussian law is degenerate, carrying no mass in the
$\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$ vertical directions of
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle}.
This degeneracy is a feature and not a defect: those are exactly the
directions along which the likelihood \eqref{eq:loglik} is constant,
so no data can ever be informative about them.

Second, \eqref{eq:MLE_CLT} contains as a by-product the rate of
convergence, since it gives
$\abs{\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}([\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n])}
= O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})$
and hence, on $\mathcal{A}_n$, that the Riemannian distance between
$[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$ and $[\bm{A}_0,s_0]$ is
$O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})$.
This is the quantitative refinement of
Proposition~\ref{thm:consistency}.

Third, no assumption whatsoever has been made on the multiplicities of the
singular values of $\bm{A}_0$.
This is worth emphasizing, because the classical route to such a theorem,
namely perturbation of the eigenvectors of $\bm{S}_n$ followed by the delta
method, requires the leading $q$ eigenvalues of $\bm{\Sigma}_0$ to be simple.
When they are not, individual principal directions are genuinely not
asymptotically normal, and yet the point $[\hat{\bm{A}}_n,\hat{s}_n]$
of $M$ still is: the quotient level limit simply does not see the
multiplicities.
The reason is Theorem~\ref{thm:DQM}, where positive definiteness of
$G_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ was obtained from
$V_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)}\cap H_{(\bm{A}_0,s_0)} = \{\bm{0}\}$ alone.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:MLE_CLT} is the input required by the optimality theory of
\cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}.
Together with the local asymptotic normality expansion of
Proposition~\ref{thm:LAN} it yields, through Le Cam's third lemma, that the
maximum likelihood estimator is locally regular in the sense of their
Definition III.5, and then their convolution theorem, Theorem III.1, and
their local asymptotic minimax theorem, Theorem III.2, identify
$G^{-1}_{[\bm{A}_0,s_0]}$ as the efficiency bound over all regular estimator
sequences; compare their Theorem V.1.
Carrying this out requires the parallel transport of $(M,g)$ and a
comparison between the transported and the coordinate errors, and is left
for the next section.
\end{remark}
