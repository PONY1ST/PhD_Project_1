\chapter{Low Dimensional Asymptotic Normality}

\section{Basic Setup}\label{sec:basic_setup}

Given a "high dimensional" data set $\{\bm{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n$
where $\bm{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ for all $i = 1, \ldots, n$,
the statistician fixes a positive integer $q < p$ 
and wishes to find the $q$ principal components.
Assuming that the data is centred (that is, with mean $\bm{0}$),
the probabilistic principal component analysis model
is a statistical model that
treats $\bm{x}_i$'s as realizations 
of independent random variables $\bm{X}_i = \bm{W}\bm{Z}_i + \bm{\epsilon_i}$,
where $\bm{W}$ is an unknown $p \times q$ loading matrix, 
$\bm{Z}_i \sim N_q\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{I}_q}$ is the latent normal variable,
and $\bm{\epsilon_i} \sim N_p\bracket{\bm{0}, \sigma^2\bm{I}_p}$
is the Gaussian noise with unknown standard deviation $\sigma > 0$,
and $(\bm{Z}_1, \ldots, \bm{Z}_n, \bm{\epsilon}_1, \ldots, \bm{\epsilon}_n)$
are all independent (for now). 
Put $\theta = (\bm{W}_{\theta}, \sigma_{\theta}^2)$ for the unknown 
parameter to estimate and $p(\bm{x}; \theta)$ 
for the density of $\bm{X}_1$, namely the density of 
$N_p\bracket{\bm{0}, \bm{W}_{\theta}\bm{W}_{\theta}^T + \sigma_{\theta}^2\bm{I}_p}$.

We now setup the quotient space structure.
Write $\Theta$ for the collection of all tuples $(\bm{W}, \sigma^2)$
such that $\bm{W}$ is a real $p \times q$ matrix with $\text{rank}(\bm{W}) = q$
and $\sigma^2 \in (0, \infty)$.
Given that the collection of all real $p \times q$ matrix with rank $q$
is an open subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{pq}$,
we consider $\Theta$ as an open subset of the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{pq+1}$,
hence a Riemannian manifold with the standard Euclidean metric

\begin{equation}\label{eq:euclidean_metric}
  g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{V},c), (\bm{U},b)} = \tr(\bm{V}\bm{U}^T) + cb,
\end{equation}
whenever $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$, $(\bm{V},c)$, $(\bm{U},b) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta \cong \mathbb{R}^{p \times q} \times \mathbb{R}$.
Next, we define $(\bm{A},s)$ and $(\bm{B}, t) \in \Theta$ 
to be equivalent when they give the same density, namely when
$\bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p = \bm{B}\bm{B}^T + t\bm{I}_p$,
and write $(\bm{A},s) \sim (\bm{B}, t)$.
The equivalence classes are denoted by $[(\bm{A},s)]$
or more briefly $[\bm{A},s]$. 
Let us denote by $O(q)$ the collection of all $q \times q$ real orthogonal matrices.
It is shown in Datta's thesis that 
$[\bm{A},s] = \{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s) \mid \bm{Q} \in O(q)\}$.
Unless otherwise mentioned, $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ is the default representative 
we pick when we write an equivalence class as $[\bm{A},s]$.
Therefore, $M := \Theta / \sim$ can be identified as the orbits 
of the right action of $O(q)$ on $\Theta$
and, given that this Lie group action is smooth, proper, and free,
the quotient manifold theorem (see \cite{Lee_2013}, Theorem 21.10) shows that there is a unique smooth 
structure making $M$ a manifold of dimension $pq+1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$ and $\pi: \Theta \to M$ 
with $\pi((\bm{A},s)) = [\bm{A},s]$ is a smooth submersion.
Moreover, this action is verticle, transitive on fibers 
and is isomrtic with respect to \eqref{eq:euclidean_metric}.
Thus, there exists a unique metric $g$ on $M$ making
$(M,g)$ a Riemannian manifold and $\pi$ a Riemannian submersion
(see \cite{Lee_2018}, Theorem 2.28), meaning that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:g_def}
  g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{V},c), (\bm{U},b)}
  = g_{[\bm{A},s]} \bracket{d\pi_{(A,s)}((\bm{V},c)), d\pi_{(A,s)}((\bm{U},b))},
\end{equation}
whenever $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$, $(\bm{V},c)$, $(\bm{U},b) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta \cong \mathbb{R}^{p \times q} \times \mathbb{R}$.
\section{Exponential Map and Logarithmic Map Computation}

In this section, we carry out some basic computations on $(M,g)$.
Working with Riemannian submersion, 
we shall as usual start with computations regarding the horizontal and verticle 
tangent spaces.
We begin with a few technical details in the computation.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:horizontal_verticle}
Fix $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$. The verticle 
tangent space
$V_{(\bm{A},s)} := \ker(d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)})$
equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$,
with $\bm{\Omega}$ ranged over all $q \times q$ skew symmetric matrices.
The horizontal tangent space $H_{(\bm{A},s)} := V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp}$
equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{V}, c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$
ranged over all $c \in \mathbb{R}$ and all $q \times q$ matrices $\bm{V}$
satisfying $\bm{V}^T \bm{A} = \bm{A}^T \bm{V}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We start with computing the verticle tangent space.
Define function $\Phi: \Theta \to \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ by 
setting, for every $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$:

\begin{equation}
  \Phi((\bm{A},s)) = \bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p.
\end{equation}

We claim that 

\begin{equation*}
  \ker(d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}) = \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}).
\end{equation*}

Before proving the claim, let us first study 
$\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$.
For $(\bm{V}, c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$, 
define a curve $\gamma : (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to \Theta$
by setting $\gamma(t) = (\bm{A}+t\bm{V}, s + tc)$.
Then:

\begin{equation*}
  d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)} ((\bm{V}, c)) = \frac{d}{dt} \mid_{t = 0} \Phi(\gamma(t)) = \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p.
\end{equation*}
If $(\bm{V}, c) \in \ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$,
then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ker_Phi_equation}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p = \bm{0}.
\end{equation}
If $\bm{u} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is any nonzero vector orthogonal to
all column vectors of $\bm{A}$, then $\bm{A}^T \bm{u} = \bm{0}$ and

\begin{equation*}
  0 = \bm{u}^T (\bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T + c\bm{I}_p) \bm{u}
  = c \, \bm{u}^T \bm{u}.
\end{equation*}
Given that $\bm{u} \neq \bm{0}$, we must have $c = 0$
and \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation} reduces to

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + \bm{V} \bm{A}^T = \bm{0}.
\end{equation}
Apply $\bm{u}$ to the right to get:

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}\bm{V}^T\bm{u} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Given that $\bm{A}$ has full rank, 
rank nullity theorem forces 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{V}^T\bm{u} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, we have proven that every vector $\bm{u}$
orthgonal to columns vectors of $\bm{A}$
will also be orthgonal to column vectors of $\bm{V}$.
This means $\bm{V}=\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$ for some 
$q \times q$ matrix $\bm{\Omega}$.
Substitute this back to \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced}
gives 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}(\bm{\Omega}^T + \bm{\Omega})\bm{A}^T = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Applying $(\bm{A}^T \bm{A})^{-1} \bm{A}^T$ 
to the left and $\bm{A}(\bm{A}^T \bm{A})^{-1}$ to the right 
yields $\bm{\Omega}^T + \bm{\Omega} = \bm{0}$.
Therefore, $\bm{\Omega}$ is skew-symmetric.
Conversely, \eqref{eq:ker_Phi_equation_reduced} 
is satisfied by $\bm{V}=\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$
with any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{\Omega}$.
We have proven that $\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$
equals to the collection of all tangent vectors 
of the form $(\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$,
with $\bm{\Omega}$ ranged over all $q \times q$ skew symmetric matrices.

We now move back to prove the claim.
Let us define 
function $F: M \to \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$
by setting $F([\bm{A},s]) = \Phi((\bm{A},s))$.
Clearly, $F$ satisfies
$F \circ \pi = \Phi$ 
and is hence a smooth injective function (here the smoothness of $F$ 
is implied by 
for example \cite{Lee_2013} Theorem 4.29).
By the chain rule, we have 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:differential_Phi}
  d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)} = d F_{[\bm{A},s]} \circ d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}.
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that 
$\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}) \subseteq \ker(d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)})$.
The other direction will follow if we can prove that 
$d F_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective.
To do so, suppose that $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ is 
such that $d F_{[\bm{A},s]} (v) = 0$.
Let $(\bm{V},c) \in T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$ be the (unique) horizontal lift of $v$ at $(\bm{A},s)$,
meaning that $(\bm{V},c)$ is the unique horizontal tangent vector at $(\bm{A},s)$
such that
 $v = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{V},c))$.
\eqref{eq:differential_Phi} then implies that 
$(\bm{V},c) \in \ker(d \Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$
and the proceeding discussion shows that 
$c = 0$ and $\bm{V} = \bm{A} \bm{\Omega}$ 
for some skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$.
To proceed, 
we look at the 
curve $\gamma: (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to \Theta$
defined by $\gamma(t) = (\bm{A}e^{t \bm{\Omega}}, s)$.
Clearly $\gamma(0)=(\bm{A}, s)$ and $\gamma'(0) = (\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)$.
Moreover, the matrix $e^{t \bm{\Omega}}$ is always orthogonal 
for any $t$ and any skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$ 
so that $\pi(\gamma(t)) = [\bm{A},s]$ for all $t$.
We have 

\begin{equation*}
  v =
  d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)) = 
  d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)} (\gamma'(0)) = \frac{d}{dt} \mid_{t=0} \pi(\gamma(t)) = \bm{0} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $d F_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective and the claim is proved.
The verticle tangent space is therefore the same as $\ker(d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)})$.

To compute the horizontal tangent space, any $(\bm{V}, c) \in V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp}$ 
will satisfy 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:orthogonal_horizontal}
  0 = g_{(\bm{A},s)} \bracket{(\bm{V}, c), (\bm{A} \bm{\Omega}, 0)} = \tr(\bm{A}^T \bm{V} \bm{\Omega})
\end{equation}
for all skew-symmetric $\bm{\Omega}$.
Decomposing $\bm{A}^T \bm{V} = \bm{S} + \bm{K}$, 
where $\bm{S}$ is the symmetric part and $\bm{K}$ is the skew-symmetric part,
we find out that 

\begin{equation*}
  0 = \tr(\bm{S} \bm{\Omega}) + \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}) = 0 + \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}) = \tr(\bm{K} \bm{\Omega}).
\end{equation*}
Setting $\bm{\Omega} = \bm{K}^T$ will yield that $\bm{K} = \bm{0}$.
Therefore, $\bm{A}^T \bm{V}$ is a symmetric matrix.
Conversely, any $\bm{V}$ such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{V}$ is symmetric will satisfy 
\eqref{eq:orthogonal_horizontal}. 
The proof is complete.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:exponential_map}
Given $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ and $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$, 
geodesics in $M$ starting at $[\bm{A},s]$ with initial velocity 
$v$ takes the format of curves 
$\gamma: I \to M$, where $I$ is an open interval containing $0$,
defined by $\gamma(t) = [\bm{A} + t\bm{V}, s + tc]$,
where $(\bm{V},c) \in H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ is the horizontal lift 
of $v$ at $ (\bm{A},s)$.
Consequently, the exponential map 
is computed as $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(v) = [\bm{A} + \bm{V}, s + c]$,
well-defined over all $v$ with small norm.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First of all, let us quickly check that the curve $\gamma$ is well defined.
Indeed, if $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$, where $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$, is another representative of $[\bm{A},s]$,
let $(\bm{V}',c')$ be the horizontal lift of $v$ to $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$.
With the function $R_{\bm{Q}}: \Theta \to \Theta$ defined by $R_{\bm{Q}}((\bm{A},s)) = (\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$,
we have 

\begin{equation*}
  v = d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)} (\bm{V},c) = d(\pi \circ R_{\bm{Q}})_{(\bm{A},s)} (\bm{V},c)
  = d \pi_{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)} (dR_{\bm{Q}})_{(\bm{A},s)}(\bm{V},c)
  = d \pi_{(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)} (\bm{V}\bm{Q},c).
\end{equation*}
Thus, $\bm{V}' = \bm{V}\bm{Q}$ and $c'=c$.
Substitute this into the definition of $\gamma$ 
will yield the exact same curve.

The fact that $\gamma$ is a geodesic follows from a well-known property of 
geodesics in case of Riemannian submersion (see for example 
\cite{O_neill_1967} Section 2 Corollary 2 or \cite{Valencia_2021} Theorem 3.6).
Any geodesic in $\Theta$ with initial velocity being horizontal 
will always have horizontal velocities and will remain a geodesic in $M$ after 
applying a Riemannian submersion. 
\end{proof}

To compute the logarithmic map, we need first preparatory lemmas that 
work out an orthogonal matrix based on given points in the space.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:q_classification}
If $\bm{L}$ is an invertible $q \times q$ matrix
and $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$ are such that $\bm{L}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric,
then there exists matrices $\bm{R}\in O(q)$, $\bm{P} \in O(q)$, 
a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ whose diagonal entries are $1$ or $-1$,
and a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{S}$ containing the singular values 
of $\bm{L}$ in increasing order 
such that $\bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$
and $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let us start by applying the singular value decomposition 
of $\bm{L}$ to get matrices $\bm{R}'\in O(q)$, $\bm{P}' \in O(q)$
and $\bm{S}$ such that $\bm{L} = \bm{R}'\bm{S}(\bm{P}')^T$.
Substituting the given condition that $\bm{L}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric will yield that
$\bm{S}\bm{J} = \bm{J}^T\bm{S}$, where $\bm{J} := (\bm{P}')^T \bm{Q} \bm{R}'$.

Clearly $\bm{J} \in O(q)$.
The equation 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{S}^2 = \bm{S}\bm{J}\bm{J}^T\bm{S} = (\bm{S}\bm{J})(\bm{S}\bm{J})^T 
  = (\bm{J}^T\bm{S})(\bm{J}^T\bm{S})^T = \bm{J}^T\bm{S}^2\bm{J}
\end{equation*}
shows that 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{J}\bm{S}^2=\bm{S}^2\bm{J}.
\end{equation*}
Looking at each entry of both size,
we end up with 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{J}_{ij}(\bm{S}_{jj}^2-\bm{S}_{ii}^2) = 0
\end{equation*}
for $i,j = 1, \ldots q$.
Given that $\bm{L}$ is invertible, its singular values are all strictly positive.
Therefore, in fact we have 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:j_s_equation}
  \bm{J}_{ij}(\bm{S}_{jj}-\bm{S}_{ii}) = 0
\end{equation}
for all $i$ and all $j$ so that $\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{S}\bm{J}=\bm{J}^T\bm{S}$.
Invert $\bm{S}$ yields that $\bm{J}$ is actually symmetric.
Being a symmetric orthogonal matrix, $\bm{J}^2 = \bm{I}_q$ and the eigenvalues of $\bm{J}$ 
must be $1$ or $-1$.
Another look at \eqref{eq:j_s_equation} shows that $\bm{J}$ is 
a block-diagonal matrix, diagonal if and only if
all diagonal entries of $\bm{S}$ are distinct.
Furthermore, each block of $\bm{J}$ must still be orthogonal and symmetric.

We now diagonalize $\bm{J}$ based on the above information.
Suppose that $\bm{S} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{s}_i\bm{I}_{m_i}$,
where $\bm{s}_1 > \ldots > \bm{s}_k$ are the unique diagonal entries of 
$\bm{S}$ and each repeats for $m_i$ times.
Thus, $\bm{J}$ has $k$ blocks.
Write the $i$th block by $\bm{J}_i$ so that
$\bm{J} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{J}_i$.
The spectral theorem then allows us to orthogonally diagonalize 
$\bm{J}_i = \bm{K}_i\bm{D}_i\bm{K}_i^T$,
where 
$\bm{K}_i \in O(m_i)$ and
$\bm{D}_i$ is an $m_i \times m_i$ diagonal matrix with $1$ or $-1$ diagonal 
entry. Put $\bm{K} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{K}_i$
and $\bm{D} = \oplus_{i=1}^k \bm{D}_i$.
Then $\bm{K} \in O(q)$, $\bm{D}$ is a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix 
with $1$ or $-1$ diagonal entry, and $\bm{J} = \bm{K}\bm{D}\bm{K}^T$.
This involved construction leads us to the outcome that 
$\bm{K}$ in fact commutes with $\bm{S}$:

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{K}\bm{S} = \bigoplus_{i=1}^k \bm{s}_i\bm{K}_i = \bm{S}\bm{K}.
\end{equation*}
Put $\bm{P} = \bm{P}'\bm{K}$,
$\bm{R} = \bm{R}'\bm{K}$.
We have 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{Q} = \bm{P}' \bm{J} (\bm{R}')^T = \bm{P}' \bm{K}\bm{D}\bm{K}^T (\bm{R}')^T
  = \bm{P} \bm{D} \bm{R}^T
\end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T = 
  \bm{R}'\bm{K} \bm{S}\bm{K}^T(\bm{P}')^T
  = \bm{R}'\bm{S} \bm{K}\bm{K}^T(\bm{P}')^T
  = \bm{R}'\bm{S} (\bm{P}')^T =\bm{L}.
\end{equation*}
This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:singular_PR}
If $\bm{L}$ is an invertible $q \times q$ matrix
with singular value decomposition $\bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$,
then 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{P}\bm{R}^T = (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{L}^T.
\end{equation*}
This means, although neither $\bm{P}$ nor $\bm{R}$ is uniquely determined,
$\bm{P}\bm{R}^T$ is uniquely determined.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Given that

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{L}^T\bm{L} = \bm{P}\bm{S}^2\bm{P}^T,
\end{equation*}
we have
\begin{equation*}
  (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}} = \bm{P}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T.
\end{equation*}
Therefore:
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{L} = \bm{R}\bm{P}^T\bm{P}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T = \bm{R}\bm{P}^T (\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
The result follows from inverting $(\bm{L}^T \bm{L})^{\frac{1}{2}}$ and take transpose.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:logarithmic_map}
For every $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ there exists an open
neighborhood $U \subset M$ containing $[\bm{A},s]$ such that

\begin{enumerate}
  \item For every $[\bm{B},t] \in U$, $\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.
  \item The logarithmic map $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}:U \to T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ 
  is well-defined and is smooth in $U$. In fact:

  \begin{equation*}
    \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})-\bm{A}, t-s)),
  \end{equation*}
  where 

  \begin{equation*}
    \bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B}) 
    = (\bm{B}^T \bm{A} \bm{A}^T \bm{B})^{- \frac{1}{2}} \bm{B}^T \bm{A} \in O(q).
  \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First of all, 
if $\bm{R} \in O(q)$ and $\bm{P} \in O(q)$,
then $\bm{Q}(\bm{A}\bm{P},\bm{B}\bm{R}) = \bm{R}^T\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})\bm{P}$
and the same computation as in the proof of 
Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map}
shows that the formula of logarithmic map is indeed well-defined.

Given that the determinant is a continuous function
and $\det(\bm{A}^T\bm{A}) > 0$,
there exists a neighborhood $U_{\Theta} \subset \Theta$ 
containing $(\bm{A},s)$ 
such that $\det(\bm{A}^T\bm{B}) > 0$ 
whenever $(\bm{B},t) \in U_{\Theta}$.
Thus,
for any $[\bm{B},t] \in U_1 := \pi(U_{\Theta}) \subset M$,
$\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.

Next, 
it is a well-known fact that $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ 
is a local diffeomorphism 
and thus there exists an open neighborhood $U_2 \subset M$ containing $[\bm{A},s]$
such that the logarithmic map $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
is well-defined and is smooth in $U_2$.

We now compute $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
for $[\bm{B},t] \in U := U_1 \cap U_2$.
Suppose that $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ 
satisfies $v = \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t])$.
By Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map},
$[\bm{B},t] = [\bm{A} + \bm{V},s+c]$,
where $(\bm{V}, c)$ is the horizontal lift of $v$ to $(\bm{A}, s)$.
It follows that $c = t-s$ and that
there exists a $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$
such that $\bm{V} = \bm{B}\bm{Q}-A$.
Or equivalently:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithmic_general_in_proof}
  \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A}, t-s)).
\end{equation}
Given that $(\bm{V}, c)$ is a horizontal tangent vector, 
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle} shows that 

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}^T(\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A}) = (\bm{B}\bm{Q}-\bm{A})^T \bm{A}
\end{equation*}
and consequently $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}\bm{Q}$ is symmetric.
Lemma~\ref{thm:q_classification} with $\bm{L} = \bm{A}^T \bm{B}$
shows the existence of $\bm{R}$, $\bm{P} \in O(q)$,
a diagonal matrix $\bm{S}$ with the singular values of $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$
in increasing order, and a $q \times q$ diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$
with diagonal entries $1$ or $-1$
such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B} = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T$
and $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$.
\eqref{eq:logarithmic_general_in_proof} now becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logarithmic_PR_in_proof}
  \text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t]) 
    = d \pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T-\bm{A}, t-s)).
\end{equation}
Now let $\bm{B} \to \bm{A}$ and $t \to s$.
The left hand size of \eqref{eq:logarithmic_PR_in_proof}
now becomes $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{A},s]) = \bm{0} \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
The singular value decomposition converges to 
the orthogonal diagonalization because $\bm{A}^T \bm{A}$
is symmetric, meaning that $\bm{P} =  \bm{R}$. Thus

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^T-\bm{A} = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{P}^T = \bm{I}_q$
and $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$.
Lemma~\ref{thm:singular_PR} then shows 
that 
\begin{equation*}
  \bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{R}^T = (\bm{B}^T \bm{A} \bm{A}^T \bm{B})^{- \frac{1}{2}} \bm{B}^T \bm{A}.
\end{equation*}
This completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Unfortunately, the logarithmic map is not globally defined
in the standard way
even over all $[\bm{B},t]$ such that $\bm{A}^T\bm{B}$ is invertible.
For a counterexample, take $p = 2$, $q = 1$,
$\bm{A} = [1,0]^T$, $\bm{B} = [1,1]^T$ and $s = t = 1$.
In this case, $O(1) = \{1,-1\}$. Then, 
although $\bm{A}^T\bm{B} = 1$ is invertible,
we still have 

\begin{equation*}
  \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))}
  = [\bm{B},1] = [-\bm{B},1]
  = \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}\bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((-\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))}.
\end{equation*}
The reason is that,
even when we assume $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible, 
deducing $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ 
relies on letting $[\bm{B},t] \to [\bm{A},s]$.
If $[\bm{B},t]$ is far away from $[\bm{A},s]$,
then $\bm{Q} = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$ with some $\bm{D} \neq \bm{I}_q$
can still produce a valid horizontal vector whose image under
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is $[\bm{B},t]$,
so that $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ fails to be injective
and admits no globally defined inverse.

Nevertheless, the choice $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ has an interesting 
property of selecting the geodesic of minimal speed
and length when $[\bm{B},t]$ is any point 
such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible.
To see this, 
consider a general candidate $\bm{Q}' = \bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$ and
write $v' = d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s))$,
so that $(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s)$ is the horizontal lift of $v'$
at $(\bm{A},s)$ and $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(v') = [\bm{B},t]$.
Let us compute the norm of $v'$:

\begin{align*}
  & \tab[0.4cm] g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v', v')
  = g_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\Theta}\bracket{(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s), (\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A}, t-s)} \\
  &= \tr\bracket{(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A})(\bm{B}\bm{Q}'-\bm{A})^T} + (t-s)^2 
  = \tr(\bm{B}\bm{B}^T) + \tr(\bm{A}\bm{A}^T) - 2\tr(\bm{S}\bm{D}) + (t-s)^2,
\end{align*}
where the last equality substituted
$\bm{A}^T\bm{B}\bm{Q}' = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{P}^T\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T = \bm{R}\bm{S}\bm{D}\bm{R}^T$
and used the cyclic property of the trace.
The fact that
the diagonal entries of $\bm{S}$ are strictly positive
show that
$g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v', v')$ is minimized precisely when $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$.
Given that the geodesic starting at $[\bm{A},s]$
with initial velocity $v'$ reaches $[\bm{B},t]$ at time $1$ with constant speed
$\sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v', v')}$, its length is $\sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v', v')}$
so that $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_q$ gives the slowest and hence shortest geodesic.
To illustrate,
let us go back to the counterexample in the previous paragraph.
Put $v_{\pm} := d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}((\pm\bm{B}-\bm{A},0))$.
Direct computation shows $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v_+,v_+) = 1$ and $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v_-,v_-) = 5$
so that the option corresponding to $\bm{D} = \bm{I}_1=1$
gives the slowest speed.
Therefore, it is actually possible to define 
$\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
over all $[\bm{B},t]$ such that $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is invertible
by manually asking it to output the minimal speed candidate 
and this definition will yield the same $\bm{Q}(\bm{A},\bm{B})$
formula. 

However, the situation totally breaks down 
in case $\bm{A}^T \bm{B}$ is not invertible.
Consider again $p =2$, $q=1$, $\bm{A} = [1,0]^T$, $s = t = 1$,
but this time $\bm{B} = [0,1]^T$.
Then $g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v_+,v_+) = g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v_-,v_-) = 2$
and there are two distinct geodesics of the same speed 
so $\text{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}([\bm{B},t])$
cannot be well-defined.

(Picture: there is a hole at $[0,0]^T$. 
Starting $\bm{A}= [1,0]^T$, as $\bm{B}= [0,1]^T$ is glued to $[0,-1]^2$,
$v_+$ points from $[1,0]^T$ to $[0,1]^T$,
$v_-$ points from $[1,0]^T$ to $[0,-1]^T$)

Given that our work should not require a global definition,
we stick to the standard definition of logarithmic map
only defined locally around $[\bm{A},s]$.
\end{remark}

\section{Properties of the Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis Model}
We now show that the model with parameter space $M$
is \textit{differentiable in quadratic mean}
in the sense developed in \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, definition 

\chapter{Draft}
We now investigate some properties of the model
when the parameter space is now $M$.
The goal of this section is to show that the model,
viewed as a family of probability measures indexed by the
quotient manifold $M$, is \emph{differentiable in quadratic mean},
and to compute its score operator and its Fisher information operator
in closed form.
This is the entry point of the Le Cam theory on parameter manifolds
developed in \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}
and is what eventually licences the asymptotic normality statement
we are after.
Throughout we place ourselves in the framework of that paper
with their parameter manifold taken to be our $M$
and their parameter of interest $\psi$ taken to be the identity,
so that no distinction between the parameter and the parameter of interest
needs to be carried along.

Let us first fix some notation.
We write $\mathrm{Sym}(p)$ for the space of real symmetric $p \times p$ matrices
and $\mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p) \subset \mathrm{Sym}(p)$ for the open convex cone
of symmetric positive definite ones.
For a real matrix $\bm{B}$ we write $\norm{\bm{B}}_F$ for its Frobenius norm,
$\norm{\bm{B}}_2$ for its spectral norm, and
$\sigma_{\min}(\bm{B})$ for its smallest singular value.
For $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ we write
$\norm{v} := \sqrt{g_{[\bm{A},s]}(v,v)}$
and $\bar{v} \in H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ for the horizontal lift of $v$ at $(\bm{A},s)$,
so that \eqref{eq:g_def} says precisely that
$v \mapsto \bar{v}$ is a linear isometry from $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ onto $H_{(\bm{A},s)}$.

\subsection*{The statistical model on \texorpdfstring{$M$}{M}}

For $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ put

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_map}
  \bm{\Sigma}(\bm{A},s) := \bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p,
\end{equation}
which lies in $\mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ because $\bm{A}\bm{A}^T \succeq \bm{0}$ and $s > 0$.
By the very definition of the equivalence relation $\sim$,
the map \eqref{eq:sigma_map} is constant on equivalence classes
and separates distinct ones.
It therefore descends to an \emph{injective} map on $M$,
which we still denote by $\bm{\Sigma}$ and evaluate as
$\bm{\Sigma}[\bm{A},s] := \bm{\Sigma}(\bm{A},s)$.
Consequently the family

\begin{equation}\label{eq:model_family}
  \mathcal{P} := \curly{P_{[\bm{A},s]} \mid [\bm{A},s] \in M},
  \qquad
  \frac{dP_{[\bm{A},s]}}{d\bm{x}}(\bm{x})
  = f(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])
  := \frac{\exp\bracket{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T \bm{\Sigma}[\bm{A},s]^{-1}\bm{x}}}
          {(2\pi)^{p/2}\det\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}[\bm{A},s]}^{1/2}},
\end{equation}
is well defined --- the density does not depend on the representative
chosen --- and the model is \emph{identifiable},
in the sense that $[\bm{A},s] \mapsto P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective.
Identifiability is exactly what was lost on $\Theta$ and
is recovered on $M$ by construction;
this is the whole point of passing to the quotient.
Following the Le Cam theory we work with the square root of the density,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:root_density}
  r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]) := \sqrt{f(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])},
\end{equation}
which is a unit vector of the Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p, d\bm{x})$
because $\int_{\mathbb{R}^p} r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])^2 \, d\bm{x} = 1$.

The next lemma records the two structural facts that make everything
in this section work:
the exponential map of $M$ is \emph{affine} in the horizontal lift
(this is the content of Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map}),
and the covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$ read through that chart
is an \emph{exact} quadratic polynomial with no remainder.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:exp_chart}
Fix $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ and put
$\rho(\bm{A},s) := \min\curly{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}), s} > 0$.
Let $h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ with $\norm{h} < \rho(\bm{A},s)$
and write its horizontal lift as $\bar{h} = (\bm{V}_h, c_h) \in H_{(\bm{A},s)}$.
Then:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h, s + c_h) \in \Theta$, so that
  $\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(h) = [\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h, s+c_h]$ is defined,
  and $h \mapsto \bar{h}$ is linear.
  \item Writing $\bm{\Sigma}_0 := \bm{\Sigma}(\bm{A},s)$ and
  $\bm{\Sigma}(h) := \bm{\Sigma}\squared{\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(h)}$,
  we have the exact identity

  \begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_of_h}
    \bm{\Sigma}(h) = \bm{\Sigma}_0 + \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] + \bm{V}_h\bm{V}_h^T,
    \qquad \text{where} \qquad
    \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\squared{(\bm{V},c)} := \bm{V}\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + c\bm{I}_p.
  \end{equation}
  The map $\bar{h} \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]$ is linear with values in $\mathrm{Sym}(p)$
  and is the differential at $(\bm{A},s)$ of the map \eqref{eq:sigma_map}.
  \item $\bm{\Sigma}(h) \succeq (s - \norm{h})\bm{I}_p \succ \bm{0}$;
  in particular $\bm{\Sigma}(h) \in \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$.
  \item The composite map $h \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]$ does not depend on
  the representative of $[\bm{A},s]$ used to define it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Since $v \mapsto \bar{v}$ is a linear isometry, it is linear and
$\norm{\bm{V}_h}_F^2 + c_h^2 = \norm{\bar{h}}^2 = \norm{h}^2$,
so that $\norm{\bm{V}_h}_2 \le \norm{\bm{V}_h}_F \le \norm{h}$ and $\abs{c_h} \le \norm{h}$.
Weyl's perturbation inequality for singular values gives

\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h) \ge \sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}) - \norm{\bm{V}_h}_2
  \ge \rho(\bm{A},s) - \norm{h} > 0,
\end{equation*}
so $\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h$ has rank $q$;
and $s + c_h \ge s - \norm{h} \ge \rho(\bm{A},s) - \norm{h} > 0$.
Hence $(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h, s+c_h) \in \Theta$ and
Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} applies.

(2) Expanding,

\begin{equation*}
  (\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h)(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h)^T + (s+c_h)\bm{I}_p
  = \underbrace{\bm{A}\bm{A}^T + s\bm{I}_p}_{\bm{\Sigma}_0}
  + \underbrace{\bm{V}_h\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{V}_h^T + c_h\bm{I}_p}_{\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]}
  + \bm{V}_h\bm{V}_h^T,
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:sigma_of_h}; there is no higher order term because
the expression is quadratic to begin with.
Linearity and symmetry of $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}$ are clear,
and differentiating $t \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}(\bm{A}+t\bm{V}, s+tc)$ at $t=0$
returns $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\squared{(\bm{V},c)}$.

(3) $\bm{\Sigma}(h) - (s+c_h)\bm{I}_p = (\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h)(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h)^T \succeq \bm{0}$
and $s + c_h \ge s - \norm{h} > 0$ by (1).

(4) Let $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$, $\bm{Q} \in O(q)$, be another representative.
The computation in the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map}
shows that the horizontal lift of $h$ at $(\bm{A}\bm{Q},s)$ is $(\bm{V}_h\bm{Q}, c_h)$.
Therefore the value of \eqref{eq:sigma_of_h} computed at that representative is

\begin{equation*}
  \bm{V}_h\bm{Q}(\bm{A}\bm{Q})^T + \bm{A}\bm{Q}(\bm{V}_h\bm{Q})^T + c_h\bm{I}_p
  = \bm{V}_h\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^T\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^T\bm{V}_h^T + c_h\bm{I}_p
  = \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}],
\end{equation*}
as claimed.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Differentiability in quadratic mean}

In the classical Euclidean theory the parameter perturbation is $\theta + h$;
on a manifold there is no such addition, and its role is played by
$\text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(h)$.
Likewise the score, which classically is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{\dim\Theta}$,
becomes a linear functional on the tangent space,
that is, an element of the cotangent space $T^*_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
The following definition is the manifold version of
differentiability in quadratic mean;
it reproduces \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, Definition III.1,
with a change of symbols,
the model tangent space being their equation (7).

\begin{definition}\label{thm:dqm_definition}
Let $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ and abbreviate
$[\bm{A},s]_h := \text{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}(h)$,
defined for $h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ of small enough norm
by Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}.
The model $\mathcal{P}$ of \eqref{eq:model_family} is said to be
\emph{differentiable in quadratic mean} (DQM) at $P_{[\bm{A},s]}$
if there exists a map

\begin{equation*}
  \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]} : \mathbb{R}^p \longrightarrow T^*_{[\bm{A},s]}M,
\end{equation*}
called the \emph{score operator},
assigning to each $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ a linear functional
$h \mapsto \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)$ on $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,
such that, as $h \to \bm{0}$ in $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dqm}
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^p}
  \bracket{
    r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h) - r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])
    - \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)\, r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])
  }^2 d\bm{x}
  = o\bracket{\norm{h}^2}.
\end{equation}
In that case we write $S_{[\bm{A},s]} := \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})$
for the \emph{score} of the model, where $\bm{X} \sim P_{[\bm{A},s]}$,
and call

\begin{equation*}
  \Lambda_{[\bm{A},s]}
  := \overline{\text{span}}\curly{S_{[\bm{A},s]}(h) \mid h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M}
  \subseteq L^2\bracket{P_{[\bm{A},s]}}
\end{equation*}
the \emph{model tangent space} at $[\bm{A},s]$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Three comments on the shape of \eqref{eq:dqm}.
First, the norm $\norm{h}$ and the space $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
in which $h$ lives are intrinsic:
no chart has been chosen, and the condition is
invariant under the choice of representative $(\bm{A},s)$ of the class.
Second, the perturbation $[\bm{A},s]_h$ is the geodesic one,
not a coordinate increment;
the whole difficulty of transporting Le Cam's theory to a manifold
usually lies here, and Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map}
is what dissolves it in the present model,
since the geodesic perturbation is affine in the horizontal lift.
Third, the score operator, if it exists, is unique:
\eqref{eq:dqm} determines the element
$\frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(h)\, r(\cdot;[\bm{A},s])$ of $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p,d\bm{x})$
as a limit of difference quotients, and $r(\cdot;[\bm{A},s]) > 0$ everywhere.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The score and the Fisher information operator}

We now produce the candidate score operator and compute
its first two moments.
The bilinear form $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ obtained below is
the Fisher information operator of \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026}, Definition III.2,
namely the covariance $\mathbb{E}\squared{S_{[\bm{A},s]}(h)\,S_{[\bm{A},s]}(h')}$
of the score, evaluated in our model.
The following elementary Gaussian identity is the only probabilistic input.

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:gaussian_quadratic}
Let $\bm{\Sigma}_1 \in \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ and let $\bm{X} \sim N_p(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_1)$.
For $\bm{\Delta} \in \mathrm{Sym}(p)$ define
$T_{\bm{\Delta}} : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:T_delta}
  T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{x})
  := \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bracket{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bm{\Sigma}_1}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}}.
\end{equation}
Then $\bm{\Delta} \mapsto T_{\bm{\Delta}}$ is linear,
$T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X})$ is square integrable with
$\mathbb{E}\squared{T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X})} = 0$, and
for all $\bm{\Delta}$, $\bm{\Delta}' \in \mathrm{Sym}(p)$

\begin{equation}\label{eq:T_delta_covariance}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X}) T_{\bm{\Delta}'}(\bm{X})}
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}'}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Linearity is clear from \eqref{eq:T_delta}.
Put $\bm{M} := \bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}$,
which is symmetric because $\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}$ and $\bm{\Delta}$ are,
and likewise $\bm{M}' := \bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}'\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}$.
Using the cyclic property of the trace twice,

\begin{equation*}
  \tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{x}\bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}}
  = \bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{x} = \bm{x}^T\bm{M}\bm{x},
  \qquad
  \tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}} = \tr(\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}),
\end{equation*}
so that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:T_as_quadratic_form}
  T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bm{M}\bm{x} - \tfrac{1}{2}\tr(\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}).
\end{equation}
Thus $T_{\bm{\Delta}}$ is a quadratic polynomial in $\bm{x}$
and is square integrable against a Gaussian law;
and $\mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{X}\bm{X}^T} = \bm{\Sigma}_1$
together with the linearity and continuity of the trace gives
$\mathbb{E}\squared{T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X})} = 0$.

For the covariance it therefore suffices, by \eqref{eq:T_as_quadratic_form}, to prove

\begin{equation}\label{eq:isserlis_consequence}
  \text{Cov}\bracket{\bm{X}^T\bm{M}\bm{X}, \, \bm{X}^T\bm{M}'\bm{X}}
  = 2\tr\bracket{\bm{M}\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}'\bm{\Sigma}_1}.
\end{equation}
Whiten by writing $\bm{X} = \bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}\bm{Z}$ with $\bm{Z} \sim N_p(\bm{0},\bm{I}_p)$,
and set $\widetilde{\bm{M}} := \bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}\bm{M}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}$
and $\widetilde{\bm{M}}' := \bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}\bm{M}'\bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}$,
both symmetric, so that
$\bm{X}^T\bm{M}\bm{X} = \bm{Z}^T\widetilde{\bm{M}}\bm{Z}$
and $\bm{X}^T\bm{M}'\bm{X} = \bm{Z}^T\widetilde{\bm{M}}'\bm{Z}$.
For independent standard normal coordinates one has Isserlis' formula

\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{Z_iZ_jZ_kZ_l} = \delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk},
\end{equation*}
which is checked directly: if some index occurs an odd number of times
the expectation vanishes and so does the right hand side;
if the four indices split into two distinct pairs
both sides equal $1$; and if all four coincide both sides equal $3$.
Hence

\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{Z}^T\widetilde{\bm{M}}\bm{Z} \cdot \bm{Z}^T\widetilde{\bm{M}}'\bm{Z}}
  &= \sum_{i,j,k,l} \widetilde{\bm{M}}_{ij}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'_{kl}\,\mathbb{E}\squared{Z_iZ_jZ_kZ_l} \\
  &= \bracket{\sum_{i}\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{ii}}\bracket{\sum_{k}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'_{kk}}
   + \sum_{i,j}\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{ij}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'_{ij}
   + \sum_{i,j}\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{ij}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'_{ji} \\
  &= \tr\widetilde{\bm{M}}\,\tr\widetilde{\bm{M}}' + 2\tr\bracket{\widetilde{\bm{M}}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'},
\end{align*}
the last equality using the symmetry of $\widetilde{\bm{M}}'$.
Since $\mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{Z}^T\widetilde{\bm{M}}\bm{Z}} = \tr\widetilde{\bm{M}}$
and similarly for $\widetilde{\bm{M}}'$, subtracting the product of the means leaves

\begin{equation*}
  \text{Cov}\bracket{\bm{X}^T\bm{M}\bm{X}, \, \bm{X}^T\bm{M}'\bm{X}}
  = 2\tr\bracket{\widetilde{\bm{M}}\widetilde{\bm{M}}'}
  = 2\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}\bm{M}\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}'\bm{\Sigma}_1^{1/2}}
  = 2\tr\bracket{\bm{M}\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}'\bm{\Sigma}_1},
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:isserlis_consequence}.
Substituting back,

\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X}) T_{\bm{\Delta}'}(\bm{X})}
  = \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2\tr\bracket{\bm{M}\bm{\Sigma}_1\bm{M}'\bm{\Sigma}_1}
  = \tfrac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}'},
\end{equation*}
which is \eqref{eq:T_delta_covariance}.

\medskip
Neither of the two steps above is new: both are special cases of moment
formulas for the matrix variate normal law collected in
\cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000}.
There a $q \times n$ random matrix satisfies
$\bm{Y} \sim N_{q,n}\bracket{\bm{M}_*, \bm{\Sigma}_* \otimes \bm{\Psi}}$
when
$\mathrm{vec}\bracket{\bm{Y}^T} \sim
N_{qn}\bracket{\mathrm{vec}\bracket{\bm{M}_*^T}, \bm{\Sigma}_* \otimes \bm{\Psi}}$
(\cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000}, Definition 2.2.1),
so the choice $q = 1$, $n = p$, $\bm{M}_* = \bm{0}$, $\bm{\Sigma}_* = 1$ and
$\bm{\Psi} = \bm{\Sigma}_1$ makes $\bm{Y}$ the row vector $\bm{X}^T$ and turns a
matrix quadratic form $\bm{Y}\bm{A}\bm{Y}^T$ into the scalar
$\bm{X}^T\bm{A}\bm{X}$;
we write $(\cdot)^T$ for the transpose that \cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000} denotes by a
prime, the prime in $\bm{M}'$ here being a label and not a transpose.
Under this dictionary \cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000}, Corollary 2.3.3.1(iii), is
Isserlis' formula stated directly for $\bm{\Sigma}_1$,

\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}\squared{X_iX_jX_kX_l}
  = (\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{ij}(\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{kl}
  + (\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{ik}(\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{jl}
  + (\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{il}(\bm{\Sigma}_1)_{jk},
\end{equation*}
which makes the whitening step unnecessary, while
\cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000}, Theorem 7.7.2, gives

\begin{equation*}
  \text{Cov}\bracket{\bm{Y}\bm{A}\bm{Y}^T, \, \bm{Y}\bm{B}\bm{Y}^T}
  = \tr\bracket{\bm{A}\bm{\Psi}\bm{B}^T\bm{\Psi}}
  + \tr\bracket{\bm{A}^T\bm{\Psi}\bm{B}^T\bm{\Psi}}
\end{equation*}
once the terms carrying the mean $\bm{M}_*$ drop out and the commutation matrix
reduces to $K_{11} = 1$;
with $\bm{A} := \bm{M}$ and $\bm{B} := \bm{M}'$, both symmetric, this is exactly
\eqref{eq:isserlis_consequence}.
The same identity can be read off from \cite{Gupta_Nagar_2000},
Theorem 7.7.1(ii), combined with the first moment
$\mathbb{E}\squared{\bm{Y}\bm{A}\bm{Y}^T} = \tr\bracket{\bm{A}^T\bm{\Psi}}$
of Theorem 2.3.5(ii) there.
We have kept the direct derivation because it is short and self contained.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:score_fisher}
Fix $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ and write $\bm{\Sigma}_0 := \bm{\Sigma}[\bm{A},s]$.
Define, for $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and $h$, $h' \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,

\begin{align}
  \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)
  &:= \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\bracket{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bm{\Sigma}_0}\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]},
  \label{eq:score} \\
  G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h')
  &:= \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]\,\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}']}.
  \label{eq:fisher}
\end{align}
Then:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item For each $\bm{x}$ the map $h \mapsto \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)$ is linear,
  so that $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x}) \in T^*_{[\bm{A},s]}M$;
  and $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
  Neither depends on the representative $(\bm{A},s)$ of the class.
  \item If $\bm{X} \sim P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ then $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h)$
  is square integrable with

  \begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}\squared{\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h)} = 0,
    \qquad
    \mathbb{E}\squared{\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h)\,\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h')}
    = G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h').
  \end{equation*}
  \item $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is positive semidefinite; more precisely,

  \begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher_frobenius}
    G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h)
    = \frac{1}{2}\norm{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}}_F^2,
  \end{equation}
  so that $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) = 0$ if and only if $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{0}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) By Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(1) and (2),
$h \mapsto \bar{h} \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]$ is a composition of linear maps,
with values in $\mathrm{Sym}(p)$;
composing with the linear map $\bm{\Delta} \mapsto T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{x})$
of Lemma~\ref{thm:gaussian_quadratic}
(taken with $\bm{\Sigma}_1 = \bm{\Sigma}_0$) gives \eqref{eq:score},
which is therefore linear in $h$.
Bilinearity of \eqref{eq:fisher} is likewise clear,
and symmetry follows from the cyclic property of the trace
together with the symmetry of $\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}$ and of $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\cdot]$:
writing $\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}']$
and transposing inside the trace exchanges $\bar{h}$ and $\bar{h}'$.
Independence of the representative is Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(4),
$\bm{\Sigma}_0$ itself being representative independent by construction.

(2) With $\bm{\Sigma}_1 := \bm{\Sigma}_0$ and $\bm{\Delta} := \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]$
we have $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(h) = T_{\bm{\Delta}}$
in the notation \eqref{eq:T_delta},
and $P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is exactly $N_p(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_0)$ by \eqref{eq:model_family}.
The claims are then Lemma~\ref{thm:gaussian_quadratic} verbatim,
the covariance formula \eqref{eq:T_delta_covariance}
being \eqref{eq:fisher}.

(3) Put $\bm{N} := \bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}$,
which is symmetric, so that
$\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2}\bm{N}\bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2}$ and
$\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}\bm{N}\bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2}$.
Squaring,
$\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}]}^2 = \bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1/2}\bm{N}^2\bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2}$,
whose trace is $\tr(\bm{N}^2) = \norm{\bm{N}}_F^2$ by cyclicity and symmetry of $\bm{N}$.
This is \eqref{eq:fisher_frobenius}.
Finally $\norm{\bm{N}}_F = 0$ forces $\bm{N} = \bm{0}$ and hence
$\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2}\bm{N}\bm{\Sigma}_0^{1/2} = \bm{0}$,
the converse being trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{thm:nondegeneracy_remark}
Part (3) reduces the nondegeneracy of the Fisher information operator
to the purely linear algebraic question of whether
$\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{0}$ forces $\bar{h} = \bm{0}$
for \emph{horizontal} $\bar{h}$.
Equivalently, writing $V_{(\bm{A},s)}$ and $H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ as in
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle}, the question is whether
$\ker\bracket{\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}} = V_{(\bm{A},s)}$ exactly,
so that $\ker\bracket{\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}} \cap H_{(\bm{A},s)} = \curly{\bm{0}}$.
This is settled in the affirmative by Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy} below,
and --- as that proof makes plain --- with no condition whatsoever
on the multiplicities of the singular values of $\bm{A}$.
Almost all of the work has in fact already been done:
the identification of $\ker(\dot{\bm{\Sigma}})$ was carried out
inside the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle},
where $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}$ appears as the differential $d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}$.
Let us also record that nondegeneracy is \emph{not} needed for the
differentiability in quadratic mean proved below,
and is not assumed there;
it becomes indispensable only later, when $G_{[\bm{A},s]}^{-1}$
is to be interpreted as an asymptotic covariance.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:nondegeneracy}
Let $[\bm{A},s] \in M$. Then $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is positive definite:
for $h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,

\begin{equation*}
  G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) = 0 \iff h = \bm{0}.
\end{equation*}
Equivalently, $\ker\bracket{\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}} = V_{(\bm{A},s)}$ exactly,
and $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}$ restricted to $H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ is injective.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The implication $\Leftarrow$ is bilinearity.
For $\Rightarrow$, suppose $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) = 0$ and
let $\bar{h} \in H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ be the horizontal lift of $h$.
By Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(3) this forces

\begin{equation}\label{eq:in_kernel}
  \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{h}] = \bm{0}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
  \bar{h} \in \ker\bracket{\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}}.
\end{equation}
Now $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}$ is by Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(2)
the differential at $(\bm{A},s)$ of the map \eqref{eq:sigma_map},
which is the map called $\Phi$ in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle};
so $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}} = d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}$
and \eqref{eq:in_kernel} reads $\bar{h} \in \ker\bracket{d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}}$.
That proof establishes, as its central claim, that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel_is_vertical}
  \ker\bracket{d\Phi_{(\bm{A},s)}} = \ker\bracket{d\pi_{(\bm{A},s)}} = V_{(\bm{A},s)},
\end{equation}
the vertical space.
Hence $\bar{h} \in V_{(\bm{A},s)}$.
But $\bar{h}$ is horizontal, and $H_{(\bm{A},s)} = V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp}$
by definition, so

\begin{equation*}
  \bar{h} \in V_{(\bm{A},s)} \cap V_{(\bm{A},s)}^{\perp} = \curly{\bm{0}}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore $\bar{h} = \bm{0}$, and since $v \mapsto \bar{v}$ is a
linear isomorphism onto $H_{(\bm{A},s)}$ we conclude $h = \bm{0}$.
The two reformulations are immediate:
\eqref{eq:kernel_is_vertical} is the first,
and the second follows from it together with
$H_{(\bm{A},s)} \cap V_{(\bm{A},s)} = \curly{\bm{0}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Two comments on the hypotheses.

First, \emph{no eigengap is used.}
Nothing in the proof refers to the singular values of $\bm{A}$,
let alone to their being distinct.
This is worth stressing because the classical route to asymptotic normality
in principal component analysis --- perturbation of the eigenvectors of the
sample covariance, followed by the delta method --- does require the
leading population eigenvalues to be simple.
The reason for the difference is that individual principal directions
are not what is being estimated here:
the point $[\bm{A},s]$ of the quotient is,
and the quotient has already absorbed the rotational ambiguity
that repeated singular values would otherwise create.

Second, \emph{the hypothesis $q < p$ is essential},
and is exactly where it enters:
the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle}
produces a nonzero $\bm{u} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ orthogonal to every column of $\bm{A}$,
which exists precisely because $\text{rank}(\bm{A}) = q < p$,
and uses it to force $c = 0$.
Without it the conclusion is false.
Indeed, suppose $q = p$, so that $\bm{A}$ is invertible,
and take any $c \neq 0$ together with
$\bm{V} := -\frac{c}{2}\bm{A}^{-T}$.
Then $\bm{A}^T\bm{V} = -\frac{c}{2}\bm{I}_p$ is symmetric,
so $(\bm{V},c)$ is horizontal by Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle},
while

\begin{equation*}
  \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}\squared{(\bm{V},c)}
  = \bm{V}\bm{A}^T + \bm{A}\bm{V}^T + c\bm{I}_p
  = -\tfrac{c}{2}\bm{I}_p - \tfrac{c}{2}\bm{I}_p + c\bm{I}_p = \bm{0},
\end{equation*}
so this nonzero horizontal vector lies in $\ker(\dot{\bm{\Sigma}})$
and $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is degenerate.
The smallest instance is $p = q = 1$, where
$\bm{\Sigma}(a,s) = a^2 + s$ maps a two dimensional parameter space
onto a one dimensional set of variances while the vertical space is trivial;
the model is then hopelessly non-identifiable even after quotienting,
which is the concrete reason the standing assumption $q < p$
of Section~\ref{sec:basic_setup} cannot be dispensed with.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{thm:why_nondegenerate}
It is worth explaining why the conclusion of
Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy} deserves to be called
\emph{nondegeneracy of the model},
and how it lines up with what that word means classically.
Everything below rests on one identity.
Anticipating Proposition~\ref{thm:dqm}, and writing
$d_H(P,Q) := \norm{r_P - r_Q}_{L^2}$ for the Hellinger distance
in the normalisation induced by \eqref{eq:root_density},
the DQM condition \eqref{eq:dqm} says exactly that
$r\bracket{\cdot\,;[\bm{A},s]_h} - r\bracket{\cdot\,;[\bm{A},s]}
= \frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(h)\,r\bracket{\cdot\,;[\bm{A},s]} + o(\norm{h})$
in $L^2$.
Since $r\bracket{\cdot\,;[\bm{A},s]}^2$ is the density of $P_{[\bm{A},s]}$,
the leading term has squared $L^2$ norm

\begin{equation*}
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^p}\bracket{\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)}^2
  r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]}^2 d\bm{x}
  = \tfrac{1}{4}\,\mathbb{E}\squared{\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h)^2}
  = \tfrac{1}{4}\,G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h)
\end{equation*}
by Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(2), so that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:hellinger_speed}
  d_H\bracket{P_{[\bm{A},s]_h},\, P_{[\bm{A},s]}}^2
  = \frac{1}{4}\,G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) + o\bracket{\norm{h}^2}.
\end{equation}
So $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h)$ is, up to the factor $\frac{1}{4}$,
the \emph{squared speed at which the distribution moves}
when the parameter is moved in the direction $h$.
Positive definiteness of $G$ therefore says:
no direction of the parameter space is invisible to the data.
That is the content, and each of the classical meanings of nondegeneracy
is a rephrasing of it.

\emph{Local identifiability.}
The model \eqref{eq:model_family} is identifiable by construction,
$[\bm{A},s] \mapsto P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ being injective.
It is essential to see that this does \emph{not} already give
Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy}:
injectivity of a smooth map does not imply injectivity of its differential,
as $t \mapsto t^3$ on $\mathbb{R}$ shows.
Identifiability is a statement about the map, nondegeneracy about its derivative,
and the latter is strictly stronger locally.
Concretely, if $G(h,h) = 0$ for some $h \neq \bm{0}$
then by \eqref{eq:hellinger_speed} the parameter can be moved at unit speed
along the geodesic $t \mapsto [\bm{A},s]_{th}$
while the distribution stays put to first order:
distinct parameters, but statistically indistinguishable at the relevant scale.

\emph{The classical regularity condition.}
In the Euclidean theory one says the model is regular at $\vartheta_0$ when
it is DQM there \emph{and} the Fisher information matrix $I_{\vartheta_0}$
is nonsingular.
Those two conditions are, in the present setting,
exactly Proposition~\ref{thm:dqm} and Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy},
so with the latter in hand we have established
the full classical regularity hypothesis on $M$.
Singularity of $I_{\vartheta_0}$ is what classically renders
the Cram\'er--Rao bound vacuous and the limiting Gaussian shift experiment
$\curly{N\bracket{h, I^{-1}_{\vartheta_0}}}$ meaningless;
here it is what would prevent the covariance $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$,
which is to be the asymptotic covariance of the estimator,
from existing at all.

\emph{The sharpest form.}
Suppose $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) = 0$ for some $h \neq \bm{0}$.
Since $G_{[\bm{A},s]}(h,h) = \mathbb{E}\squared{\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})(h)^2}$
by Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(2),
the score in the direction $h$ vanishes almost surely.
The local asymptotic normality expansion in the direction $h$,
whose two terms are the score sum and $-\frac{1}{2}G(h,h)$,
would then reduce to $o_{P}(1)$:
the log-likelihood ratio between the perturbed and unperturbed experiments
converges to zero, so the two are asymptotically indistinguishable
and no test can have asymptotic power against the alternative $h$.
Degeneracy is thus not a technical inconvenience
but the assertion that a genuine direction of the parameter space
carries no information whatsoever.

\emph{The quotient as a cure.}
There is a purely linear algebraic way to see what passing to $M$ accomplished.
A positive semidefinite form on a vector space $E$
descends to a positive \emph{definite} form on the quotient $E/\ker$,
and this is the standard repair for a degenerate quadratic form.
Formula \eqref{eq:fisher} makes sense for an arbitrary,
not necessarily horizontal, tangent vector of $\Theta$,
and so defines a Fisher form on $T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta$;
by \eqref{eq:fisher_frobenius} and \eqref{eq:kernel_is_vertical}
that form is positive semidefinite with kernel exactly $V_{(\bm{A},s)}$,
of dimension $\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$.
Hence the model on $\Theta$ \emph{is} degenerate in the classical sense,
and every classical theorem is simply inapplicable there.
Passing to the quotient is precisely the operation of dividing out
the null space of the Fisher form, since
$T_{(\bm{A},s)}\Theta / V_{(\bm{A},s)} \cong H_{(\bm{A},s)} \cong T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$;
and the content of Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy}
is that the division is exactly right, neither too coarse nor too fine.
This is the precise sense in which the quotient manifold $M$,
and not $\Theta$, is the correct parameter space for an asymptotic theory.

\emph{A metric restatement.}
Combining \eqref{eq:hellinger_speed} with
Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy} and the compactness of the unit
sphere of $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$, there are constants
$0 < c \le C < \infty$ and $\delta > 0$ with

\begin{equation*}
  c\,\norm{h} \;\le\; d_H\bracket{P_{[\bm{A},s]_h},\, P_{[\bm{A},s]}} \;\le\; C\,\norm{h},
  \qquad \norm{h} < \delta .
\end{equation*}
That is, the statistical distance between two nearby members of the model
is comparable to the Riemannian distance between the corresponding points of $M$.
The two inequalities fail in opposite ways and both are fatal.
The upper bound is the DQM half:
it fails for the uniform family of the examples at the end of
Section~\ref{sec:dqm_geometry}, where
$d_H \asymp \norm{h}^{1/2} \gg \norm{h}$ and the model moves too fast,
which is why estimation there is $n$-consistent rather than $\sqrt{n}$-consistent.
The lower bound is the nondegeneracy half:
it fails when $G$ is singular, the model then moving too slowly
in some direction for any $\sqrt{n}$-consistent estimator to exist there.
The regular theory lives exactly between the two.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The model is differentiable in quadratic mean}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:dqm}
The model $\mathcal{P}$ of \eqref{eq:model_family} is differentiable in quadratic mean,
in the sense of Definition~\ref{thm:dqm_definition},
at $P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ for \emph{every} $[\bm{A},s] \in M$,
with score operator $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ given by \eqref{eq:score}.
Consequently the score $S_{[\bm{A},s]} = \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{X})$ is
centred and square integrable,
its covariance --- the Fisher information operator of the model at $[\bm{A},s]$ ---
is the bilinear form $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ of \eqref{eq:fisher},
and the model tangent space is the finite dimensional subspace

\begin{equation*}
  \Lambda_{[\bm{A},s]}
  = \curly{\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(h) \mid h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M}
  \subseteq L^2\bracket{P_{[\bm{A},s]}},
  \qquad \dim \Lambda_{[\bm{A},s]} \le \dim M.
\end{equation*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix a representative $(\bm{A},s) \in \Theta$ of the base point,
which is legitimate since by Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(4)
and Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(1) nothing in the statement depends on it.
Abbreviate $\bm{\Sigma}_0 := \bm{\Sigma}(\bm{A},s)$,
$\rho := \min\curly{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A}), s} > 0$, and $d := \dim M$.
Throughout, $h$ ranges over the open ball
$B := \curly{h \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M \mid \norm{h} < \rho/2}$,
on which Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart} applies.
Write, for $h \in B$ and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:chart_loglik}
  L(h;\bm{x}) := \log f\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h}
  = -\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log\det\bm{\Sigma}(h)
  - \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\bm{x},
\end{equation}
and $r(h;\bm{x}) := r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h} = \exp\bracket{\frac{1}{2}L(h;\bm{x})}$,
where $\bm{\Sigma}(h) \in \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ is as in Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(2)--(3).
Note that $r(\bm{0};\cdot) = r(\cdot;[\bm{A},s])$.

We stress at the outset the structural point on which the whole proof rests:
by Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} the geodesic perturbation
$[\bm{A},s]_h$ is \emph{affine} in the horizontal lift $\bar{h}$,
so that $\curly{P_{[\bm{A},s]_h} \mid h \in B}$ is an ordinary parametric family
indexed by an open subset of the $d$ dimensional Euclidean space
$H_{(\bm{A},s)} \cong T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
No approximation of the exponential map is involved anywhere below;
the manifold statement \eqref{eq:dqm} \emph{is} the classical
quadratic mean differentiability of this Euclidean family at $h = \bm{0}$,
that is, condition (7.1) of \cite{van_der_Vaart_1998}, page 93.
One could therefore finish by quoting a standard sufficient condition,
namely \cite{van_der_Vaart_1998}, Lemma 7.6,
which asks that $h \mapsto \sqrt{f\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h}}$
be continuously differentiable for every $\bm{x}$
and that the Fisher information be well defined and continuous in $h$;
Steps 1 to 4 below verify precisely these two hypotheses.
We nevertheless carry the argument through to the end in Step 5,
both to keep the section self-contained
and because the passage from the directionwise statement to the
uniform one, invisible in the one dimensional phrasing of the quoted lemma,
is the only genuinely delicate point.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: pointwise smoothness in $h$ and the derivative.}
By \eqref{eq:sigma_of_h} the map $h \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}(h)$
is a polynomial of degree two with values in $\mathrm{Sym}(p)$,
hence $C^{\infty}$, and since $h \mapsto \bar{h}$ is linear,
its directional derivative at $h \in B$ in the direction $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
is, writing $\bar{v} = (\bm{U},b)$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_dot_at_h}
  D_v \bm{\Sigma}(h)
  = \bm{U}\bracket{\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h}^T + \bracket{\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h}\bm{U}^T + b\bm{I}_p
  =: \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}] \in \mathrm{Sym}(p),
\end{equation}
obtained by differentiating $t \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}(h+tv)$ at $t=0$
and using $\overline{h+tv} = \bar{h} + t\bar{v}$.
In particular $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\bm{0}}[\bar{v}] = \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bar{v}]$,
and $v \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]$ is linear for each fixed $h$.

On the open cone $\mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ the maps
$\bm{\Sigma} \mapsto \log\det\bm{\Sigma}$ and $\bm{\Sigma} \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$
are $C^{\infty}$, with differentials
$\bm{\Delta} \mapsto \tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}}$ and
$\bm{\Delta} \mapsto -\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$ respectively.
By Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(3), $\bm{\Sigma}(h)$ stays in that cone for $h \in B$.
Therefore, for every fixed $\bm{x}$, the map $h \mapsto L(h;\bm{x})$ is $C^{\infty}$ on $B$,
hence so is $h \mapsto r(h;\bm{x}) = \exp\bracket{\frac{1}{2}L(h;\bm{x})}$,
and the chain rule gives

\begin{align}
  D_v L(h;\bm{x})
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]}
   + \frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\bm{x}
   \notag \\
  &= \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\bracket{\bm{x}\bm{x}^T - \bm{\Sigma}(h)}\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]},
  \label{eq:chart_score}
\end{align}
where the second equality uses the cyclic property of the trace
exactly as in \eqref{eq:T_as_quadratic_form}, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:root_derivative}
  D_v r(h;\bm{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,D_v L(h;\bm{x})\, r(h;\bm{x}).
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:chart_score} at $h = \bm{0}$ with \eqref{eq:score} we see that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:score_is_derivative}
  D_v L(\bm{0};\bm{x}) = \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(v),
\end{equation}
so the candidate score of Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher} is
indeed the pointwise derivative of the log-likelihood in the chart.
It is convenient to abbreviate, for $h \in B$ and $v \in T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi_def}
  \phi_h^v(\bm{x}) := D_v L(h;\bm{x})\, r(h;\bm{x}),
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  D_v r(h;\cdot) = \tfrac{1}{2}\phi_h^v
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \phi_{\bm{0}}^v = \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(v)\, r(\cdot;[\bm{A},s]).
\end{equation}
By linearity of $v \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]$ and of the trace,
$v \mapsto \phi_h^v$ is linear for each fixed $h$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: $\phi_h^v$ lies in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p, d\bm{x})$, with a closed form norm.}
Fix $h \in B$ and $v$. Since $r(h;\cdot)^2$ is the $N_p(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}(h))$ density,

\begin{equation*}
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \phi_h^v(\bm{x})^2 \, d\bm{x}
  = \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \bracket{D_v L(h;\bm{x})}^2 f\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h}\,d\bm{x}
  = \mathbb{E}\squared{T_{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{X}_h)^2},
\end{equation*}
where $\bm{X}_h \sim N_p(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}(h))$, and where we applied
\eqref{eq:chart_score} to recognise $D_v L(h;\cdot) = T_{\bm{\Delta}}$
in the notation \eqref{eq:T_delta} of Lemma~\ref{thm:gaussian_quadratic}
taken with $\bm{\Sigma}_1 := \bm{\Sigma}(h)$ and $\bm{\Delta} := \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]$.
Note that Lemma~\ref{thm:gaussian_quadratic} is applied here with a
\emph{general} symmetric $\bm{\Delta}$; no horizontality of $\bar{v}$
relative to the shifted representative $(\bm{A}+\bm{V}_h, s+c_h)$ is needed,
and indeed none is available.
That lemma therefore gives $\phi_h^v \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^p,d\bm{x})$ with

\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi_norm}
  \norm{\phi_h^v}_{L^2}^2
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]\,\bm{\Sigma}(h)^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]}
  =: I(h)(v,v),
\end{equation}
the Fisher information of the chart family at $h$;
by \eqref{eq:fisher} and \eqref{eq:score_is_derivative},
$I(\bm{0})(v,v) = G_{[\bm{A},s]}(v,v) = \norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: $h \mapsto \phi_h^v$ is $L^2$ continuous at $h=\bm{0}$, for each fixed $v$.}
Let $(h_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be any sequence in $B$ with $h_n \to \bm{0}$.

\emph{(a) Pointwise convergence.}
The map $h \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}(h)$ is continuous by \eqref{eq:sigma_of_h},
takes values in $\mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ by Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(3),
and both $\bm{\Sigma} \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}^{-1}$ and $\bm{\Sigma} \mapsto \det\bm{\Sigma}$
are continuous there;
also $h \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_h[\bar{v}]$ is affine by \eqref{eq:sigma_dot_at_h}.
Reading off \eqref{eq:chart_loglik}, \eqref{eq:chart_score} and \eqref{eq:phi_def},
the function $\phi_h^v(\bm{x})$ is built from these by
sums, products, traces and the exponential, so that
for \emph{every} $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$,

\begin{equation*}
  \phi_{h_n}^v(\bm{x}) \longrightarrow \phi_{\bm{0}}^v(\bm{x}), \qquad n \to \infty.
\end{equation*}

\emph{(b) Convergence of the norms.}
By the same continuity applied to the closed form \eqref{eq:phi_norm},

\begin{equation*}
  \norm{\phi_{h_n}^v}_{L^2}^2 = I(h_n)(v,v) \longrightarrow I(\bm{0})(v,v) = \norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2 .
\end{equation*}

\emph{(c) From (a) and (b) to $L^2$ convergence.}
This is Riesz's criterion, which we include for completeness.
Since $(\alpha - \beta)^2 \le 2\alpha^2 + 2\beta^2$ for reals, the functions

\begin{equation*}
  \psi_n := 2\bracket{\phi_{h_n}^v}^2 + 2\bracket{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}^2 - \bracket{\phi_{h_n}^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}^2
\end{equation*}
are nonnegative, and by (a) they converge pointwise to $4\bracket{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}^2$.
Fatou's lemma and then (b) give

\begin{align*}
  4\norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2
  &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \liminf_{n} \psi_n \, d\bm{x}
  \;\le\; \liminf_{n} \int_{\mathbb{R}^p} \psi_n \, d\bm{x} \\
  &= \liminf_{n} \bracket{2\norm{\phi_{h_n}^v}_{L^2}^2 + 2\norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2
     - \norm{\phi_{h_n}^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2} \\
  &= 4\norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2 - \limsup_{n} \norm{\phi_{h_n}^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2,
\end{align*}
the last equality because the first two terms form a convergent sequence.
As $\norm{\phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2} < \infty$ by Step 2, we may cancel it
and conclude $\limsup_n \norm{\phi_{h_n}^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2}^2 \le 0$,
that is, $\phi_{h_n}^v \to \phi_{\bm{0}}^v$ in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p, d\bm{x})$.

The sequence being arbitrary, the nondecreasing function

\begin{equation}\label{eq:omega_v}
  \omega_v(\delta) := \sup_{\norm{h} \le \delta} \norm{\phi_h^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2},
  \qquad 0 < \delta < \rho/2,
\end{equation}
satisfies $\omega_v(\delta) \to 0$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$.
Indeed, being nondecreasing it has a limit $\lambda \ge 0$ at $0^+$;
if $\lambda > 0$ we could choose, for each $n$ large, some $h_n$ with
$\norm{h_n} \le 1/n$ and $\norm{\phi_{h_n}^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v}_{L^2} > \lambda/2$,
contradicting what we just proved.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 4: uniformity over directions.}
Let $e_1, \ldots, e_d$ be a $g_{[\bm{A},s]}$ orthonormal basis of $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$.
By Step 1 the map $v \mapsto \phi_h^v$ is linear for each fixed $h$,
and so therefore is $v \mapsto \phi_h^v - \phi_{\bm{0}}^v$.
Hence for a unit vector $u = \sum_{j=1}^d u_j e_j$,
for which $\sum_j u_j^2 = 1$ and so $\sum_j \abs{u_j} \le \sqrt{d}$
by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
the triangle inequality in $L^2$ gives

\begin{equation*}
  \norm{\phi_h^u - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u}_{L^2}
  = \norm{\sum_{j=1}^d u_j\bracket{\phi_h^{e_j} - \phi_{\bm{0}}^{e_j}}}_{L^2}
  \le \sum_{j=1}^d \abs{u_j}\,\norm{\phi_h^{e_j} - \phi_{\bm{0}}^{e_j}}_{L^2}
  \le \sqrt{d}\,\max_{1 \le j \le d}\norm{\phi_h^{e_j} - \phi_{\bm{0}}^{e_j}}_{L^2}.
\end{equation*}
Taking suprema and invoking Step 3 at each of the finitely many basis vectors,
the modulus of continuity

\begin{equation}\label{eq:omega}
  \omega(\delta) := \sup_{\norm{h} \le \delta}\ \sup_{\norm{u} = 1} \norm{\phi_h^u - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u}_{L^2}
  \;\le\; \sqrt{d}\,\max_{1 \le j \le d} \omega_{e_j}(\delta)
\end{equation}
is nondecreasing and satisfies $\omega(\delta) \to 0$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$.
This is where the finite dimensionality of $M$ enters the analysis:
it is what upgrades the directionwise continuity of Step 3
to the uniformity over directions that \eqref{eq:dqm} demands,
and the argument would have to be run differently
on an infinite dimensional parameter manifold.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 5: the fundamental theorem of calculus and Minkowski's inequality.}
Let $h \in B$ with $h \ne \bm{0}$ and set $u := h/\norm{h}$, a unit vector.
For $t$ in the open interval $\bracket{-\rho/(2\norm{h}), \rho/(2\norm{h})} \supset [0,1]$
the point $th$ lies in $B$, so that $t \mapsto r(th;\bm{x})$ is defined there,
and it is $C^1$ --- indeed $C^{\infty}$ --- for each fixed $\bm{x}$,
because $h' \mapsto r(h';\bm{x})$ is so by Step 1.
We claim that

\begin{equation}\label{eq:t_derivative}
  \frac{d}{dt} r(th;\bm{x})
  \;\overset{(a)}{=}\; D_h r(th;\bm{x})
  \;\overset{(b)}{=}\; \norm{h}\, D_u r(th;\bm{x})
  \;\overset{(c)}{=}\; \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\,\phi_{th}^u(\bm{x}).
\end{equation}

For $(a)$, recall that $D_v$ denotes the directional derivative
in the direction $v$ \emph{at the point indicated by its argument}, that is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:directional_derivative_recalled}
  D_v r(h';\bm{x}) := \frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0} r(h'+sv;\bm{x}).
\end{equation}
This is meaningful because the parameter $h'$ ranges over an open subset
of the \emph{vector space} $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,
in which a point and a direction are the same kind of object
and the sum $h' + sv$ makes sense;
we are working inside the exponential chart precisely so that this is so.
Now observe that the curve $t \mapsto th$ is a straight line
traversed at the constant velocity $h$,
so that incrementing its parameter $t$ by $s$
increments its value by exactly $sh$, and hence

\begin{equation*}
  \frac{d}{dt} r(th;\bm{x})
  = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{r\bracket{(t+s)h;\bm{x}} - r(th;\bm{x})}{s}
  = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{r\bracket{th+sh;\bm{x}} - r(th;\bm{x})}{s}
  = D_h r(th;\bm{x}),
\end{equation*}
the last expression being, by \eqref{eq:directional_derivative_recalled}
with $h' = th$ and $v = h$, exactly the defining difference quotient
of $D_h$ evaluated at the point $th$.
The two derivatives in $(a)$ are thus the same limit written twice.
This is of course the chain rule applied to
$r(\cdot\,;\bm{x}) \circ \gamma$ with $\gamma(t) = th$ and $\gamma'(t) \equiv h$,
but because $\gamma$ is affine no chain rule is actually needed:
the middle equality above is the identity $(t+s)h = th + sh$
and nothing more.
Note also that the point at which the derivative is taken
moves with $t$, whereas the direction $h$ does not;
this is what makes $\phi^u_{th}$, rather than $\phi^u_{\bm{0}}$,
appear on the right of \eqref{eq:t_derivative}.

Equality $(b)$ is linearity in the direction.
By \eqref{eq:chart_score} the direction $v$ enters $D_vL(h';\bm{x})$
only through the linear map $v \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_{h'}[\bar{v}]$,
so $v \mapsto D_vL(h';\bm{x})$ is linear, as was noted at the end of Step 1;
by \eqref{eq:root_derivative} the factor $r(h';\bm{x})$ does not depend on $v$,
so $v \mapsto D_v r(h';\bm{x}) = \frac{1}{2}D_vL(h';\bm{x})\,r(h';\bm{x})$
is linear as well.
Applying this at $h' = th$ to the decomposition $h = \norm{h}\,u$
gives $(b)$.
Finally $(c)$ is \eqref{eq:root_derivative} combined with
the definition \eqref{eq:phi_def} of $\phi^u_{th}$,
which say together that $D_u r(h';\cdot) = \frac{1}{2}\phi^u_{h'}$.

Integrating \eqref{eq:t_derivative} from $0$ to $1$,

\begin{equation*}
  r(h;\bm{x}) - r(\bm{0};\bm{x}) = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\int_0^1 \phi_{th}^u(\bm{x})\,dt .
\end{equation*}
On the other hand $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h) = \norm{h}\,\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(u)$
by linearity, so that by \eqref{eq:phi_def}

\begin{equation*}
  \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)\, r(\bm{x};[\bm{A},s])
  = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\,\phi_{\bm{0}}^u(\bm{x})
  = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\int_0^1 \phi_{\bm{0}}^u(\bm{x})\,dt.
\end{equation*}
Subtracting, the DQM remainder is exactly

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dqm_remainder}
  R_h(\bm{x})
  := r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h} - r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]}
   - \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)\,r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]}
   = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\int_0^1 \bracket{\phi_{th}^u(\bm{x}) - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u(\bm{x})}dt.
\end{equation}
Abbreviate the integrand of \eqref{eq:dqm_remainder} by

\begin{equation*}
  F(t,\bm{x}) := \phi_{th}^u(\bm{x}) - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u(\bm{x}),
  \qquad (t,\bm{x}) \in [0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^p,
\end{equation*}
so that \eqref{eq:dqm_remainder} reads
$R_h = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\int_0^1 F(t,\cdot)\,dt$;
here $h$, and with it the unit vector $u = h/\norm{h}$, is fixed,
and only $t$ and $\bm{x}$ vary.
Recall Minkowski's integral inequality in the form we need:
if $(T,\nu)$ and $(Y,\mu)$ are $\sigma$-finite measure spaces
and $F$ is $\nu \otimes \mu$ measurable, then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:minkowski}
  \bracket{\int_{Y}\abs{\int_{T} F(t,\bm{y})\,d\nu(t)}^2 d\mu(\bm{y})}^{1/2}
  \;\le\;
  \int_{T}\bracket{\int_{Y}\abs{F(t,\bm{y})}^2 d\mu(\bm{y})}^{1/2} d\nu(t),
\end{equation}
that is,
$\norm{\int_{T} F(t,\cdot)\,d\nu(t)}_{L^2(\mu)}
\le \int_{T}\norm{F(t,\cdot)}_{L^2(\mu)}\,d\nu(t)$.
This is nothing but the triangle inequality with the finite sum
replaced by an integral: the norm of an average is at most
the average of the norms.
Equivalently, it is the bound
$\norm{\int_{T} G} \le \int_{T}\norm{G}$
for the Bochner integral of the $L^2(\mu)$ valued map $G(t) := F(t,\cdot)$;
we state it in the scalar form \eqref{eq:minkowski}
to avoid having to set up vector valued integration.

We apply \eqref{eq:minkowski} with $T = [0,1]$ and $Y = \mathbb{R}^p$,
both carrying Lebesgue measure and both $\sigma$-finite.
Its hypotheses are met:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Joint measurability.} By Step 1 the map
  $(h',\bm{x}) \mapsto \phi_{h'}^u(\bm{x}) = D_uL(h';\bm{x})\,r(h';\bm{x})$
  is jointly continuous on $B \times \mathbb{R}^p$,
  being assembled from the continuous maps
  $h' \mapsto \bm{\Sigma}(h')^{-1}$ and $h' \mapsto \dot{\bm{\Sigma}}_{h'}[\bar{u}]$,
  the polynomial $\bm{x} \mapsto \bm{x}\bm{x}^T$, and the exponential.
  Precomposing with the continuous $t \mapsto th$ and subtracting the
  ($t$-independent, continuous) function $\phi_{\bm{0}}^u$
  shows that $F$ is jointly continuous, hence jointly measurable.
  \item \emph{The right hand side of \eqref{eq:minkowski} is well defined.}
  The function $\abs{F}^2$ is nonnegative and jointly measurable by (1),
  so Tonelli's theorem makes
  $t \mapsto \int_{\mathbb{R}^p}\abs{F(t,\bm{x})}^2 d\bm{x} = \norm{F(t,\cdot)}_{L^2}^2$
  a measurable function of $t$.
  \item \emph{Finiteness.} Each $F(t,\cdot)$ lies in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p,d\bm{x})$,
  since $\phi_{th}^u$ and $\phi_{\bm{0}}^u$ do by Step 2.
\end{enumerate}
Inequality \eqref{eq:minkowski} therefore gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:minkowski_applied}
  \norm{R_h}_{L^2}
  = \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\norm{\int_0^1 F(t,\cdot)\,dt}_{L^2}
  \;\le\; \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\int_0^1 \norm{\phi_{th}^u - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u}_{L^2} dt.
\end{equation}
It remains to bound the integrand of \eqref{eq:minkowski_applied}
uniformly in $t$, and this is exactly what Step 4 was arranged to provide.
For every $t \in [0,1]$ we have $\norm{th} = t\norm{h} \le \norm{h}$,
while $u$ is a unit vector;
so the pair $(th, u)$ is one of those over which the supremum
defining $\omega\bracket{\norm{h}}$ in \eqref{eq:omega} is taken, whence

\begin{equation*}
  \norm{\phi_{th}^u - \phi_{\bm{0}}^u}_{L^2} \le \omega\bracket{\norm{h}}
  \qquad \text{for all } t \in [0,1].
\end{equation*}
The integrand of \eqref{eq:minkowski_applied} is thus dominated by
a constant, and $\int_0^1 \omega\bracket{\norm{h}}\,dt = \omega\bracket{\norm{h}}$,
so that

\begin{equation*}
  \norm{R_h}_{L^2} \;\le\; \frac{\norm{h}}{2}\,\omega\bracket{\norm{h}}.
\end{equation*}
Squaring,

\begin{equation*}
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^p}
  \bracket{r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]_h} - r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]}
    - \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\bm{x})(h)\,r\bracket{\bm{x};[\bm{A},s]}}^2 d\bm{x}
  \;\le\; \frac{\norm{h}^2}{4}\,\omega\bracket{\norm{h}}^2 ,
\end{equation*}
and since $\omega(\norm{h}) \to 0$ as $h \to \bm{0}$ by Step 4,
the right hand side is $o\bracket{\norm{h}^2}$.
This is precisely \eqref{eq:dqm}, so $\mathcal{P}$ is differentiable
in quadratic mean at $P_{[\bm{A},s]}$ with score operator $\dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 6: the remaining assertions.}
The base point $[\bm{A},s] \in M$ was arbitrary, so DQM holds everywhere on $M$;
observe that the only properties of $(\bm{A},s)$ used were
$\text{rank}(\bm{A}) = q$ and $s > 0$, which hold throughout $\Theta$ by definition.
That $S_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is centred and square integrable with covariance $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$
is Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(2).
Finally, by Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(1) the map
$h \mapsto \dot{\ell}_{[\bm{A},s]}(\cdot)(h)$ is linear from $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$
into $L^2\bracket{P_{[\bm{A},s]}}$, so its range is a linear subspace
of dimension at most $\dim T_{[\bm{A},s]}M = \dim M$.
A finite dimensional subspace of a normed space is closed,
so the range already equals its own closure
and no completion is needed in the definition of $\Lambda_{[\bm{A},s]}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
It is worth isolating what made the proof go through,
because it is special to this model and not a general phenomenon
on quotient manifolds.
Quadratic mean differentiability is a statement about a
\emph{one parameter family of perturbations} of the true measure,
and on a manifold the natural perturbation is geodesic.
In general one must then control the difference between the
geodesic perturbation and any coordinate perturbation, which costs an
extra approximation argument.
Here Proposition~\ref{thm:exponential_map} says that the geodesics of $M$
are the projections of straight lines in $\Theta$, so that
$h \mapsto [\bm{A},s]_h$ is affine in the horizontal lift;
and Lemma~\ref{thm:exp_chart}(2) says that the covariance,
read in that chart, is an exact quadratic polynomial in $h$ with no remainder.
The family $\curly{P_{[\bm{A},s]_h}}$ is then a perfectly ordinary
Euclidean exponential type family, and the only genuine analysis left
is the $L^2$ continuity of Step 3.
By contrast, positive definiteness of $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$
(Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy}) is where the quotient structure
really bites: it is the one statement in this section
that would be false on $\Theta$.
\end{remark}

\section{The Geometric Content of Differentiability in Quadratic Mean}
\label{sec:dqm_geometry}

Definition~\ref{thm:dqm_definition} is short but opaque on first reading:
it is not obvious where the square root comes from,
why the perturbation is squared and integrated,
why the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ appears,
or what the condition is really asking of the model.
This section answers those questions.
The short answer is that
\emph{differentiability in quadratic mean is nothing other than
Fr\'echet differentiability, at the point in question,
of the map sending a parameter to the square root of its density,
regarded as a map into the Hilbert space $L^2$}.
Everything else in the definition is bookkeeping around that one idea.

Most of this section is expository and is not used later.
Two points are not: the warning of
Remark~\ref{thm:two_metrics} that $M$ carries two
distinct and non-proportional bilinear forms,
and the factorization of Proposition~\ref{thm:factorization},
which is the cleanest way to see what
Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy} really says.

\subsection*{The root density map}

Let us first strip away the manifold and recall the classical picture.
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ be open, let $\mu$ be a measure on a sample space $\mathcal{X}$,
and let $\curly{p_{\vartheta} \mid \vartheta \in U}$ be a family of $\mu$-densities.
Put $\mathcal{H} := L^2(\mathcal{X},\mu)$, a real Hilbert space, and define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:root_map}
  \Phi : U \longrightarrow \mathcal{H}, \qquad \Phi(\vartheta) := r_{\vartheta} = \sqrt{p_{\vartheta}}.
\end{equation}
The classical DQM condition at $\vartheta_0$ reads

\begin{equation}\label{eq:classical_dqm}
  \int_{\mathcal{X}} \bracket{r_{\vartheta_0+h} - r_{\vartheta_0}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{\vartheta_0}(h)\, r_{\vartheta_0}}^2 d\mu = o\bracket{\norm{h}^2},
\end{equation}
which, since the integral of a square is exactly a squared $\mathcal{H}$-norm,
is the same as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:frechet}
  \norm{\Phi(\vartheta_0+h) - \Phi(\vartheta_0) - D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(h)}_{\mathcal{H}}
  = o\bracket{\norm{h}},
  \qquad D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(h) := \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}_{\vartheta_0}(h)\, r_{\vartheta_0}.
\end{equation}
That is precisely the definition of Fr\'echet differentiability of $\Phi$ at $\vartheta_0$
with derivative the bounded linear map $D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}$.
The same remark applies verbatim to \eqref{eq:dqm},
with $U$ replaced by $M$ and $\mu$ by Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^p$.

\begin{remark}
It is worth saying explicitly that \eqref{eq:classical_dqm}
is a \emph{first order} condition, notwithstanding the exponent $2$
on both sides.
The square on the left is the square of the Hilbert norm
and the square on the right compensates for it;
after taking square roots one is left with \eqref{eq:frechet},
an ordinary first order Taylor statement.
The condition is stated in the squared form only because
the left hand side is then an integral one can actually estimate.
\end{remark}

Three features of the definition now explain themselves.

\emph{Why the square root.}
Because $\int r_{\vartheta}^2 \, d\mu = \int p_{\vartheta} \, d\mu = 1$,
the map $\Phi$ takes its values in the \emph{unit sphere}

\begin{equation*}
  S(\mathcal{H}) := \curly{u \in \mathcal{H} \mid \norm{u}_{\mathcal{H}} = 1},
\end{equation*}
a fixed subset of a fixed Hilbert space, the same one for every $\vartheta$.
The normalisation constraint on a density,
which is an awkward affine side condition if one works with $p_{\vartheta}$ itself,
becomes a piece of geometry.
Moreover $\norm{r_{\vartheta_1} - r_{\vartheta_2}}_{\mathcal{H}}$
is exactly the Hellinger distance between $P_{\vartheta_1}$ and $P_{\vartheta_2}$
up to the normalising convention,
so DQM says that $\vartheta \mapsto P_{\vartheta}$ is differentiable
\emph{in Hellinger distance}.
That is the metric one wants, because it interacts correctly with
products of experiments and hence survives the localisation $h \mapsto h/\sqrt{n}$
that the whole asymptotic theory is built on.

\emph{Why the choice of $\mu$ does not matter.}
Suppose $d\mu = v \, d\mu'$ for a positive $v$.
Then $p_{\vartheta}' = p_{\vartheta} v$ and $r_{\vartheta}' = r_{\vartheta}\sqrt{v}$,
and the map $u \mapsto u\sqrt{v}$ is a linear isometry
from $L^2(\mu)$ onto $L^2(\mu')$, since
$\int \abs{u \sqrt{v}}^2 d\mu' = \int u^2 v \, d\mu' = \int u^2 d\mu$.
This isometry does not depend on $\vartheta$,
so it carries \eqref{eq:frechet} to the corresponding statement for $\mu'$
and carries $\frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}(h)r_{\vartheta}$ to $\frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}(h)r_{\vartheta}'$,
leaving the score $\dot{\ell}$ itself untouched.
This is why \cite{Sun_Lin_Liu_2026} can say that the dominating measure
is not necessarily unique without any further comment.

\emph{Why the factor $\frac{1}{2}$, and why the derivative is written as a multiple of $r_{\vartheta_0}$.}
The $\frac{1}{2}$ is the chain rule for the square root:
where things are smooth enough to compute pointwise,

\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \vartheta} \sqrt{p_{\vartheta}}
  = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{p_{\vartheta}}}\,\frac{\partial p_{\vartheta}}{\partial \vartheta}
  = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial \log p_{\vartheta}}{\partial \vartheta}\,\sqrt{p_{\vartheta}}.
\end{equation*}
So the factor is there for exactly one reason:
to make the object called the score the derivative of $\log p_{\vartheta}$
rather than of $\sqrt{p_{\vartheta}}$.
As for the shape $\frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}(h)\,r_{\vartheta_0}$,
note that $u \mapsto u \, r_{\vartheta_0}$ is an isometry
from $L^2(P_{\vartheta_0})$ into $L^2(\mu)$, because

\begin{equation}\label{eq:score_isometry}
  \int \bracket{u \, r_{\vartheta_0}}^2 d\mu = \int u^2 p_{\vartheta_0} \, d\mu
  = \mathbb{E}\squared{u(\bm{X})^2}.
\end{equation}
Insisting that the derivative be written in the form
$\frac{1}{2}\dot{\ell}(h) \, r_{\vartheta_0}$
rather than merely as some element of $L^2(\mu)$
is therefore a change of variables which factors the derivative
through that isometry.
It is what makes the score a \emph{random variable},
square integrable under $P_{\vartheta_0}$ and independent of $\mu$,
instead of a $\mu$-dependent function.

\subsection*{The model as a surface in the unit sphere}

The picture to keep in mind is that a statistical model is a
parametrised surface drawn on the unit sphere of a Hilbert space,
and that the score is its tangent vector.
Two standard facts are immediate consequences of that picture rather than
of any computation with integrals.

First, \emph{the score has mean zero because the derivative is tangent to the sphere}.
Differentiating the identity $\norm{r_{\vartheta}}^2 \equiv 1$ in the direction $h$ gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:tangency}
  0 = 2\big\langle D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(h), \, r_{\vartheta_0}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
  = \int \dot{\ell}_{\vartheta_0}(h)\, r_{\vartheta_0}^2 \, d\mu
  = \int \dot{\ell}_{\vartheta_0}(h)\, p_{\vartheta_0} \, d\mu
  = \mathbb{E}\squared{\dot{\ell}_{\vartheta_0}(h)}.
\end{equation}
So the vanishing of the expected score is not an artifact of
differentiating under the integral sign,
and does not need the interchange to be justified:
it is the constraint $\norm{r_{\vartheta}} \equiv 1$ differentiated once.
This is why the classical theory obtains it as a
\emph{consequence} of DQM rather than as a hypothesis
(see \cite{van_der_Vaart_1998}, Theorem 7.2).
In our own development we verified it by direct computation
in Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(2), which is a perfectly good proof,
but \eqref{eq:tangency} is the reason.

Second, \emph{the Fisher information is the pullback of the $L^2$ inner product}.
Indeed, by \eqref{eq:score_isometry},

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fisher_is_pullback}
  \big\langle D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(h), \, D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(h')\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
  = \frac{1}{4}\int \dot{\ell}(h)\dot{\ell}(h')\, p_{\vartheta_0} \, d\mu
  = \frac{1}{4}\, G_{\vartheta_0}(h,h').
\end{equation}
So the Fisher form is four times the first fundamental form of the model
regarded as a surface in $S(\mathcal{H})$.
In particular

\begin{equation*}
  G_{\vartheta_0} \text{ is positive definite}
  \iff D\Phi_{\vartheta_0} \text{ is injective}
  \iff \Phi \text{ is an immersion at } \vartheta_0,
\end{equation*}
which is the geometric reading of Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy}:
that proposition says exactly that our model,
viewed through $\Phi$, is an immersed surface in $S(\mathcal{H})$
rather than one that has been crushed in some direction.

\subsection*{Fr\'echet, not merely directional}

It is worth being precise about which notion of differentiability is meant,
because that is where the analytic work actually lies.
Condition \eqref{eq:frechet} is \emph{Fr\'echet} differentiability:
the remainder is $o(\norm{h})$ as $h \to \bm{0}$ in any manner whatsoever.
It is strictly stronger than the directional, or Gateaux, statement

\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{t \to 0}\ \frac{1}{t}\norm{\Phi(\vartheta_0 + tv) - \Phi(\vartheta_0) - t\, D\Phi_{\vartheta_0}(v)} = 0
  \qquad \text{for each fixed } v,
\end{equation*}
which does not suffice for local asymptotic normality
even when the directional derivative happens to be linear in $v$.
The gap between the two is exactly a uniformity over directions.
This is what the classical sufficient condition
(\cite{van_der_Vaart_1998}, Lemma 7.6) buys with its
continuity hypothesis on the information matrix,
and it is what Step 4 of the proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:dqm}
supplies by hand, by combining linearity in the direction
with convergence at finitely many basis directions.
A reader who wonders why that proof is not simply Step 3
now has the answer: Step 3 alone gives only the Gateaux statement.

\subsection*{What changes on a manifold}

Almost nothing, conceptually.
Comparing \eqref{eq:dqm} with \eqref{eq:classical_dqm},
the target Hilbert space is untouched;
only the \emph{source} has changed, from an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^k$
to the manifold $M$.
Differentiability at a point of a map from a manifold into a Banach space
is an entirely standard notion,
and its derivative is a linear map $d\Phi_{[\bm{A},s]} : T_{[\bm{A},s]}M \to \mathcal{H}$.

The exponential map enters for one reason only:
on a manifold there is no ``$\vartheta_0 + h$'',
so to compare $\Phi$ at nearby points one must pass through some chart,
and $\mathrm{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is the canonical intrinsic choice of one.
It is a convenience, not an ingredient.
Concretely, if $\varphi$ is any other $C^1$ chart centred at $[\bm{A},s]$
whose differential at the origin is the identity of $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$,
then $\varphi$ and $\mathrm{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ differ by a map tangent to the identity,
which a remainder of size $o(\norm{h})$ cannot detect;
so DQM through $\varphi$ holds if and only if DQM through $\mathrm{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ holds,
and the score operator is the same in both cases.
For the same reason the condition does not depend on which
norm is used to measure $\norm{h}$,
all norms on the finite dimensional space $T_{[\bm{A},s]}M$ being equivalent.
The definition is thus considerably more robust than its
notation suggests;
what is genuinely intrinsic in it is the pair
$\bracket{T_{[\bm{A},s]}M, \; d\Phi_{[\bm{A},s]}}$.

\begin{remark}\label{thm:two_metrics}
This robustness comes with a trap worth flagging,
since $M$ now carries \emph{two} bilinear forms which are easily conflated.
The quotient Euclidean metric $g$ of \eqref{eq:g_def}
is the one that defines $\mathrm{Exp}_{[\bm{A},s]}$, $\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$,
the horizontal lift and the norm $\norm{h}$ appearing in \eqref{eq:dqm}.
The Fisher form $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ of \eqref{eq:fisher}
is the statistical one, and it is the inverse $G^{-1}_{[\bm{A},s]}$
that will appear as an asymptotic covariance.
These two are \emph{not} proportional:
$g$ is a fixed Euclidean object,
while $G$ depends on $\bm{\Sigma}_0$ through
$\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}[\cdot]\bm{\Sigma}_0^{-1}$.
Geodesics, logarithms and parallel transport are taken with respect to $g$;
variances are read off $G^{-1}$.
Nothing above is harmed by this, precisely because DQM is insensitive
to the choice of $g$, but the distinction has to be maintained
in any statement that mixes the two.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The present model: a factorization}

For the probabilistic principal component analysis model
the root density map admits a factorization that isolates
where the geometry actually lives.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:factorization}
Let $N : \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p) \to L^2(\mathbb{R}^p, d\bm{x})$
send a positive definite matrix to the square root of the
$N_p(\bm{0}, \cdot)$ density,

\begin{equation*}
  N(\bm{\Sigma}_1)(\bm{x})
  := \frac{\exp\bracket{-\frac{1}{4}\bm{x}^T\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{x}}}
          {(2\pi)^{p/4}\det(\bm{\Sigma}_1)^{1/4}}.
\end{equation*}
Then the root density map $\Phi = r(\cdot\,;\cdot)$ of \eqref{eq:root_density}
factors as $\Phi = N \circ \bm{\Sigma}$, that is,

\begin{equation*}
  M \xrightarrow{\ \bm{\Sigma}\ } \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p) \xrightarrow{\ N\ } S\bracket{L^2(\mathbb{R}^p,d\bm{x})},
\end{equation*}
with $\bm{\Sigma}$ as in \eqref{eq:sigma_map}.
Moreover $N$ is an injective immersion, and the metric it induces
on $\mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine_invariant}
  4\big\langle dN_{\bm{\Sigma}_1}[\bm{\Delta}], \, dN_{\bm{\Sigma}_1}[\bm{\Delta}']\big\rangle_{L^2}
  = \frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\,\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}'},
\end{equation}
namely one half of the affine invariant metric of the positive definite cone.
Consequently $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is the pullback along $\bm{\Sigma}$ of \eqref{eq:affine_invariant},
and

\begin{equation*}
  G_{[\bm{A},s]} \text{ is positive definite}
  \iff \bm{\Sigma} : M \to \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p) \text{ is an immersion at } [\bm{A},s].
\end{equation*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The factorization is the definition \eqref{eq:model_family} of the model,
which produces the density from the class $[\bm{A},s]$
only through the matrix $\bm{\Sigma}[\bm{A},s]$.
Injectivity of $N$ holds because distinct covariances give distinct
centred Gaussian laws.
For \eqref{eq:affine_invariant}, apply \eqref{eq:fisher_is_pullback}
to the family $\curly{N_p(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_1)}$ parametrised by
$\bm{\Sigma}_1 \in \mathrm{Sym}_{++}(p)$ itself,
whose score in the direction $\bm{\Delta}$ is $T_{\bm{\Delta}}$
in the notation \eqref{eq:T_delta}
and whose information is therefore
$\frac{1}{2}\tr\bracket{\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}\bm{\Sigma}_1^{-1}\bm{\Delta}'}$
by Lemma~\ref{thm:gaussian_quadratic}.
That form is positive definite by the computation of
Lemma~\ref{thm:score_fisher}(3) applied at $\bm{\Sigma}_1$,
so $dN$ is injective and $N$ is an immersion.
The final two claims follow by the chain rule
$d\Phi = dN \circ d\bm{\Sigma}$:
comparing \eqref{eq:fisher} with \eqref{eq:affine_invariant}
identifies $G_{[\bm{A},s]}$ as $\bm{\Sigma}^*$ of the latter,
and since $dN$ is injective, $d\Phi_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is injective
if and only if $d\bm{\Sigma}_{[\bm{A},s]}$ is.
\end{proof}

The point of Proposition~\ref{thm:factorization} is that the second arrow
is model independent: it is the same for any family of centred Gaussians,
and it is an immersion for trivial reasons.
All of the content specific to probabilistic principal component analysis
sits in the first arrow $\bm{\Sigma}$, which is why the score \eqref{eq:score},
the Fisher form \eqref{eq:fisher} and the chart covariance \eqref{eq:sigma_of_h}
are all expressed through $\dot{\bm{\Sigma}}$ and nothing else.
It also puts Remark~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy_remark} in its proper light.
On the total space $\Theta$ the map $\bm{\Sigma}$ is \emph{not} an immersion:
its differential kills the vertical space $V_{(\bm{A},s)}$,
of dimension $\frac{q(q-1)}{2}$, as was established inside the proof of
Proposition~\ref{thm:horizontal_verticle}.
That failure \emph{is} the non-identifiability of the model.
Passing to the quotient $M = \Theta/\!\sim$ is exactly the operation
that repairs it, and the assertion that the repair is complete ---
that nothing beyond the vertical space is killed ---
is precisely Proposition~\ref{thm:nondegeneracy}.
In the language of this section, then,
that proposition says that $\bm{\Sigma}$ descends from a map which is
merely constant on orbits to one which is an \emph{immersion} of $M$,
and hence that $\Phi = N \circ \bm{\Sigma}$ realises $M$ as an
immersed submanifold of the unit sphere of $L^2(\mathbb{R}^p, d\bm{x})$.

\subsection*{Two calibrating examples}

Finally, two classical one dimensional examples show that
the Fr\'echet in $L^2$ reading is doing real work,
and in particular that DQM is neither implied by nor implies
pointwise smoothness of the density.

The double exponential location family
$p_{\vartheta}(x) = \frac{1}{2}e^{-\abs{x-\vartheta}}$ on $\mathbb{R}$
is differentiable in quadratic mean at every $\vartheta$,
with score $\dot{\ell}_{\vartheta}(x) = \text{sign}(x - \vartheta)$,
even though $x \mapsto p_{\vartheta}(x)$ fails to be differentiable at $x = \vartheta$
for every $\vartheta$.
A single non-differentiability, being a $\mu$-null event,
is invisible to an $L^2$ norm.

Conversely, let $p_{\vartheta} = \vartheta^{-1}\bm{1}_{[0,\vartheta]}$ be
the uniform family on $[0,\vartheta]$, $\vartheta > 0$.
For $h > 0$ small,

\begin{equation*}
  \norm{r_{\vartheta+h} - r_{\vartheta}}^2_{L^2}
  = \int_0^{\vartheta}\bracket{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta+h}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}}^2 dx
  + \int_{\vartheta}^{\vartheta+h} \frac{dx}{\vartheta+h}
  = \frac{h^2}{4\vartheta^2} + O(h^3) + \frac{h}{\vartheta+h},
\end{equation*}
so that $\norm{r_{\vartheta+h} - r_{\vartheta}}_{L^2} \sim \sqrt{h/\vartheta}$.
The curve $\vartheta \mapsto r_{\vartheta}$ is therefore only H\"older
of exponent $\frac{1}{2}$ in $\mathcal{H}$ and is nowhere differentiable,
so no score operator can exist and the family is not DQM at any $\vartheta$ ---
notwithstanding that $p_{\vartheta}(x)$ is a perfectly smooth,
indeed constant, function of $\vartheta$ for each fixed $x \neq \vartheta$.
This is the analytic shadow of the familiar fact that the maximum
likelihood estimator in this model converges at rate $n^{-1}$
rather than $n^{-1/2}$,
and it shows that the whole asymptotic theory really is keyed
to the smoothness of a curve in a Hilbert space
and not to the smoothness of a density.
